# The Steelman

A second-year medical student writing a chapter about what his profession gets wrong should expect a specific response, and I would rather meet it here than have it follow the book around.

The response is: *you haven't seen anything yet.* You have not watched a patient go down the cascade. You have not sat with someone through the biopsy that found nothing and the complication that followed it. You have read about incidentalomas; you have not caused one. Come back in ten years and see whether you still think the profession is being precious about data.

That objection is not a dodge. It is the strongest thing anyone can say to me, and part of it is simply true. My views are high on theory and low on clinical reality, and I expect them to change as I spend more time in hospitals. Anyone who tells you their opinions survived contact with the wards unchanged is either lying or wasn't paying attention.

But "you'll understand later" is not an argument. It is a prediction about my future mental state, and it has the awkward property of being unfalsifiable in the present. If it turns out I'm wrong, I want to be shown wrong with evidence rather than seniority. So this chapter takes the four strongest objections to the previous one, states each in the best form I can manage, and answers them on the merits. Where the objection wins, I say so.

## The incidentaloma problem is real, and I have underweighted it

**The objection.** The incidentaloma critique isn't theoretical hand-wringing. Aggressive screening of asymptomatic populations can produce net harm through the cascade of interventions on clinically insignificant findings. A shadow on a scan becomes a follow-up scan, which becomes a biopsy, which becomes a bleed, which becomes a hospitalization, in a person who was never going to be troubled by the thing that started it. This isn't doctors being backwards. It is evidence-based concern about iatrogenic harm, and it is the concern that most reliably separates people who have done this work from people who have read about it.

**Where it wins.** Entirely, on the facts. The cascade is real, it is documented, and it hurts people who would otherwise have been fine. Nothing in the previous chapter should be read as denying it. If the choice were between the current regime and a world where every asymptomatic adult gets a whole-body scan and the findings are managed the way findings are managed today, the current regime is safer. I would pick it too.

**Where I still disagree.** The critique assumes that current management protocols for ambiguous findings are fixed. They are not. The cascade is a protocol-design failure, and it is being treated as an inherent property of information.

Watch how the same argument would have sounded in 2001. Physicians could have said, accurately: continuous glucose data will show fluctuations that don't matter, patients will panic, they will over-treat, and some of them will be harmed by the over-treatment. That prediction wasn't crazy. What we did about it, eventually, was develop better interpretation guidelines, better patient education, and better decision thresholds — we solved the protocol problem rather than refusing the data. Nobody now argues the correct response was to withhold glucose readings from diabetics until the interpretation problem was solved in advance.

The incidentaloma cascade deserves the same treatment. If whole-body imaging produces management challenges, the answer is better management protocols. Treating the cascade as a reason not to look is a category error dressed as clinical wisdom.

And the harm calculus remains suspiciously one-sided. Every analysis of incidentaloma risk carefully quantifies the harms of false positives and downstream interventions. The harms of missed diagnoses due to information scarcity are systematically underweighted, because they are invisible — we never learn about the cancer that would have been caught early, the aneurysm that would have been monitored, the condition that was preventable until it wasn't. Absence of evidence gets treated as evidence of absence. "We don't have randomized trials proving whole-body screening improves outcomes" becomes "therefore assume it's harmful" rather than "therefore study it properly." That asymmetry is not a finding. It is a habit.

## The CGM analogy doesn't fully transfer

**The objection.** Continuous glucose monitoring worked because diabetes has clear, actionable thresholds and because frequent monitoring demonstrably improves outcomes. Whole-body screening has no comparable evidence base. The question isn't "is data good?" — it's "does this specific data source improve outcomes net of harms?" That is an empirical question, and it has to be answered separately for every modality. Generalizing from CGMs to imaging is exactly the kind of move a person makes when they have a thesis to defend.

**Where it wins.** On the demand for modality-by-modality evidence, completely. I am not arguing that every monitoring technology deserves adoption because one did. The previous chapter said each modality must prove itself and I meant it. If whole-body screening turns out to produce net harm in properly designed trials, it should not be standard, and I will have been wrong about that modality.

**Where I still disagree.** The objection proves my point more than it refutes it. Yes, CGMs had to demonstrate outcome improvements through studies, and they did. But the establishment resisted *running those studies* because of prior assumptions about too much data. The resistance came before the evidence phase, not in response to it. That is the cultural problem I'm naming.

"Whole-body imaging doesn't have the same evidence base yet" is technically true and quietly misleading. The question is whether the default posture should be *probably harmful until proven otherwise* or *worth studying rigorously*. Medicine currently defaults to the first, and the default itself suppresses the evidence generation that would settle the matter. That is a closed loop, and closed loops are not neutral.

There is also a subtler problem with the claim that diabetes has clear actionable thresholds. It has them now. Some of them we learned *from* continuous monitoring — the technology revealed patterns we didn't know to look for, including nocturnal patterns that quarterly testing structurally could not see. You cannot always know in advance which information will prove actionable, which is the entire epistemic case for exploratory data collection. Requiring a modality to demonstrate actionability before you are allowed to gather the data that would demonstrate it is not rigor.

## The false-positive math is not medical conservatism

**The objection.** Screen a low-prevalence condition in an asymptomatic population and most positives will be false. This is basic epidemiology. A test with ninety-five percent specificity sounds excellent until you apply it to a million healthy people and generate fifty thousand false alarms, each attached to a frightened person and a follow-up pathway. The math here is real, not a cultural artifact, and no amount of enthusiasm about abundance repeals Bayes.

**Where it wins.** The math is the math, and any argument that ignores it disqualifies itself. This is also, not coincidentally, the objection I am least qualified to wave away — the arithmetic doesn't care how confident I am. Anyone making my argument owes the reader an acknowledgment that positive predictive value in low-prevalence populations is genuinely brutal, and that the intuition "more testing finds more disease" is false in exactly the way the objection says.

**Where I still disagree.** Three things.

First, we already accept false-positive burdens whenever we judge the benefit worth it. Mammography in average-risk women generates substantial false positives. We do it anyway, because the lives saved are judged to outweigh the harms. PSA screening generates a large false-positive rate, and the debate has been about whether the tradeoff is favorable, not whether false positives exist. The question is never "do false positives exist." It is "does the benefit-harm ratio favor testing" — and we cannot know that ratio for a new modality without gathering enough data to compute it.

Second, the objection assumes we are stuck with current sensitivity and specificity profiles and current management protocols. Better imaging, better biomarkers, better interpretation, better guidelines for handling ambiguous findings all move the ratio. Treating today's operating characteristics as permanent is precisely the static thinking the previous chapter was about.

Third, the false-positive argument is applied selectively, and the selectivity is revealing. Nobody argues we should stop checking vitals every four hours in hospitals on the grounds that most abnormal readings are false alarms. We accept those false positives because we believe catching the true positives matters. The interesting question is why the same reasoning doesn't extend to other abundant data sources, and I don't think the answer is epidemiological.

## "First, do no harm" is a real principle, not a rhetorical shield

**The objection.** Framing clinical caution as a status game or a training artifact is a cheap move. Physicians hold back because they have internalized an obligation not to hurt people, which is the oldest and best thing about the profession. Recasting that obligation as institutional self-protection is both unfair and corrosive, and it is the kind of argument someone makes when they want permission to do something the obligation forbids.

**Where it wins.** As a warning about tone, and I take it seriously enough to have rewritten parts of the previous chapter because of it. When I wrote that I was embarrassed by physicians dismissing a new scanner, that was a real reaction, and it was also uncharitable in a way I would resent if aimed at me. Most physicians resisting new data sources are not protecting status. They are protecting patients with the tools and time they have, inside a system that gives them fifteen minutes and holds them liable for what they miss. Any version of my argument that requires assuming bad faith is a weaker version.

**Where I still disagree.** "First, do no harm" applies to interventions. That is what it has always meant. Getting an image is not an intervention. Looking at your own glucose data is not an intervention. Having continuous vitals monitored is not an intervention. The harm arrives through subsequent action taken in response to information — which is exactly why the data/action distinction is doing real work rather than playing with words.

The principle has been quietly extended into *first, gather no information that might lead someone to act*, and the extension has never been argued for, only inherited. You can gather abundantly and still exercise complete restraint about what you do next. Those are separable, and separating them is not a loophole. It is the point.

## What would change my mind

Stating this seems only fair, since I have spent two chapters asking the profession to hold its beliefs to a standard.

If well-designed trials of multi-cancer early detection show net harm at population scale — not just false positives, but worse mortality or morbidity in the screened arm — the abundance argument loses its most important case, and I should say so publicly rather than retreating to the position that the protocols were implemented badly.

If patient access to raw data demonstrably produces worse outcomes at scale — more anxiety-driven utilization, worse adherence, worse health — then the paternalism I've been criticizing is empirically justified and I am wrong about one of the things I feel most strongly about.

If interpretation layers turn out not to work — if the summarization problem proves genuinely hard rather than merely unbuilt, so that abundant data remains unusable inside a real clinical encounter for another decade — then the practical case collapses even if the principle survives, and a principle that cannot be practiced is not much of a principle.

I don't expect these. But I have written them down, dated, in a book, which is a more expensive way to be wrong than a post I could quietly edit. That seemed like the right price to pay for spending a chapter telling other people to hold their beliefs loosely.

## Where this leaves the argument

Two of the four objections land hard. The incidentaloma cascade is real and I underweighted it. The false-positive math is real and does not care about my thesis. The other two — that the CGM analogy doesn't transfer, and that clinical caution is being unfairly recast — are partly right about tone and, I think, wrong about substance.

What survives is narrower than what I started with, which is what should happen when you actually argue against yourself rather than performing it.

Gather abundantly. Act selectively. Require each modality to prove itself, and apply the same standard of proof to the claim that scarcity is safe. Build the interpretation layer instead of using its absence as a reason to refuse the data. And stop describing the fifteen-year delay as prudence after the fact, because that is the step that makes the next fifteen-year delay inevitable.

That is a smaller claim than "medicine is anti-abundance and should stop." It is also one I can defend from where I'm standing, which the larger one wasn't.
