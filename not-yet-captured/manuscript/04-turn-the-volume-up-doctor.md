# Turn the Volume Up, Doctor

There's a moment in an old podcast episode that captures the fault line between the entrepreneurial world and the medical establishment better than anything I could construct.

One of the hosts was describing a period of founder paralysis — the specific inertia where you have the capital and the energy but can't decide what to build next. He mentioned it to his trainer, who stopped him mid-sentence.

*You are the product. The product is just you pushed out. So do you, but turn the volume knob up.*

If you're a founder, an engineer, or a marketer, that sentence lands like a green light you were already looking for. It means stripping away corporate abstraction and relying on something more direct.

If you're a physician, the same sentence sounds like a structural threat. It triggers something close to an allergic reaction.

I've thought about why for a long time, and this chapter is the answer. It's also, unavoidably, the book's own permission slip — the argument that justifies its existence, which means the reader should watch me carefully here, because I have an obvious interest in reaching a particular conclusion.

## The institutional cult of anonymity

Medicine trains invisibility on purpose, and the conditioning starts on day one.

Consider Joel Bervell, a medical student who began producing short videos on clinical blind spots — racial bias in pulse oximetry, in lung function algorithms. Objectively vital public health information, exactly the kind of evidence translation the public needs. His institutional advisors warned him to think twice before speaking publicly about hot-button issues. [^1]

The warning wasn't about accuracy. Nobody disputed the data, and the data was not marginal. Sjoding and colleagues, examining nearly fifty thousand paired measurements, found Black patients had close to three times the rate of occult hypoxemia missed by pulse oximetry as white patients. Among readings showing an oxygen saturation of 92 to 96 percent, actual arterial saturation was below 88 percent in twelve percent of measurements in Black patients and four percent in white patients. [^2]

A device in every hospital room in the country was systematically less accurate for some patients than for others. The objection was never that he had the science wrong. It was that he was visible at all — and in the legacy paradigm, visibility itself is treated as a risk-management failure.

And the conditioning starts long before it reaches the internet. We are trained toward a sterile emotional distance from patients under the banner of objective professionalism. The system wants the physician to be an interchangeable node — a white coat, a stethoscope, an institutional logo. No edge, no distinct perspective, no volume.

## What silence actually costs

This isn't merely outdated. It's actively harmful, and the mechanism is simple.

When medicine codes silence as a virtue, it doesn't stop people from seeking medical information. It only ensures they won't get it from us. Treating public visibility as a professionalism violation created an enormous information vacuum.

Vacuums fill.

Because actual physicians are afraid of institutional friction, the public square went to biohackers, wellness influencers, and people selling unvetted supplements. They have no clinical training and complete mastery of the volume knob. That's the whole trade: we kept our professional decorum and handed over the audience.

When a licensed physician decides that staying invisible is the safe career move, they are not protecting patients. They're outsourcing the public's health education to the loudest, least qualified voices available.

Invisibility is not a neutral ethical stance. It's a dereliction.

## The second reason for silence, which is newer

There's a force pushing the same direction that almost nobody in medicine is discussing, and it will get worse.

When we built the internet, the premise was that information would spread further and faster than ever. Publishing knowledge was a straightforward win: you gained reputation, built an audience, helped people, and staked a kind of intellectual claim.

AI is quietly inverting that incentive. If you develop something genuinely new — an idea, a technique, a way of doing something — there's a growing chance that publishing it doesn't make you known. It makes you redundant. You've handed it to a system that never sleeps, never forgets, and can deliver your insight to anyone who asks, instantly, without attribution.

I don't think this changes what physicians should do. But I think it explains something about why the vacuum will keep widening, and it means the argument for speaking has to be made on different grounds than it was ten years ago. The reputational return on sharing expertise is falling. The public need for it isn't.

So the case for visibility can't rest on what it does for your career. It has to rest on what happens if nobody does it.

## A workable definition of professionalism

The current standard is incoherent. Write a peer-reviewed paper three people read and you're a scientist. Summarize that same paper in plain English for a hundred thousand people and you're a self-promoter.

A better test comes from a thought experiment I've found genuinely clarifying: how would you behave if there were zero possibility of punishment or reward?

Authenticity, on that definition, is the collapse of the gap between public and private persona. There's no difference between who you are when the microphones are off and who you are when they're live.

That gives a physician two usable filters instead of vague institutional hand-wringing.

**The clinical alignment test.** Would I say this exact thing to a patient sitting across from me, using my genuine clinical judgment? If yes, then staying quiet online isn't professional discipline. It's fear wearing the costume of ethics.

**The performance filter.** Am I saying this only because it performs for an algorithm, or projects a confidence I don't actually have? If yes, *that's* the real professional violation — and notice it has nothing to do with volume. It's about deceit.

Those two tests do the work the profession's current norms pretend to do, and they have the advantage of being answerable.

## Blue links

There's a story about the early web I keep returning to.

When Marc Andreessen was asked why hyperlinks are blue, the answer was that he liked blue. No committee, no study, no consensus. One person's preference, stamped onto the architecture of the internet, and now billions of people live inside a world colored by it.

The story is trivial on the surface and not underneath. It reveals the actual scaffolding of culture: it isn't built by invisible forces or natural law. It's built by people who asserted that their preference should become reality.

Most people, faced with that, flinch — because admitting the world is shaped by people who stepped forward means admitting you could have. It's easier to believe in inevitability, in systems and institutions and the way things are, and to call the resulting passivity humility or realism.

Medicine is unusually good at manufacturing that flinch. It teaches deference as a professional virtue, which is correct in a clinical hierarchy where a confident junior can kill someone, and then extends it into every domain including ones where deference costs lives rather than saving them.

Every guideline, every standard, every norm this profession operates under was once somebody's argument. They're not weather.

## What I'm doing here

I should be direct about the self-interest, because a chapter arguing that physicians should speak publicly, written by a physician-in-training publishing a book, is not a neutral document.

I write because the intersection of technology, medicine, and systems thinking is where my mind goes when nobody is paying me to think about anything. I would write it with zero readers. That's the only test I trust for whether the work is real, and it's the one I'd apply to anyone making this argument, including me.

The credentials on a wall were never meant to be a shield to hide behind. They were meant to be a foundation to build on.

The physicians actually changing the landscape aren't inventing a persona. They're taking the clinical judgment, clear communication, and lack of jargon they already use to help people, and pushing it outward.

That's the whole ask. Stop letting institutional inertia set your reach. The product is your judgment.

Turn the volume up.
