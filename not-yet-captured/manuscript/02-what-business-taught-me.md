# What Business Taught Me That Medical School Never Could

Medical school teaches you how to identify a problem. Business taught me how to solve one.

That's the shortest way I can put the difference, and like most short formulations it's unfair to both. Medicine solves problems constantly and at a level of precision business never approaches — no startup has ever had to be right about a potassium level. But the *kind* of problem each discipline trains you to see is genuinely different, and the gap between them is the whole reason this book exists.

This chapter is about what specifically transfers. Not attitude, not confidence, not the vague entrepreneurial energy people mean when they say a doctor is "business-minded." A set of concrete analytical habits that medical training doesn't install, that I use on every argument in the rest of this book, and that the reader will need in order to follow what I'm doing.

## Individuals inside systems

In medical school you're trained to gather information, analyze data, recognize patterns, and intervene with precision. It's a beautiful craft and I don't want to be glib about it.

But if you stop there, you end up treating individuals inside broken systems — systems nobody taught you to see, let alone repair. You become extremely good at the part of the problem that fits inside a room.

Business filled in that gap for me. It taught me to think in systems, to design around friction, to align incentives, and to notice that "how things move" is itself a subject you can study. It also taught me something that sounds obvious and isn't: you don't need to be a policy expert or a billionaire to change how something works. You need to understand the mechanism, and then build something that moves it better.

## The four habits

Here is what actually transfers, stated as precisely as I can manage.

**Read the incentive before the stated reason.** In business you learn quickly that what people say they want and what the structure rewards them for are different things, and that when the two conflict the structure wins. Not because people are dishonest — because incentives operate below the level of stated intention. This is the single most useful analytical habit I have, and it's the engine of Chapter Five. When someone tells you a policy exists for patient safety, that may be entirely true. It is also a claim you can check, by asking who benefits from the policy and what would change if it disappeared.

**Treat "that's how it's done" as a claim rather than a fact.** Every organization carries practices whose original justification expired quietly. In a startup you learn to ask why constantly, partly because there's no institutional memory to defer to and partly because inherited practice is usually where the waste is hiding. Medicine has enormous institutional memory, which makes this question feel impertinent rather than routine. It's the same question.

**Follow the money to the behavior.** If you want to know why a workflow looks the way it does, look at what gets reimbursed. This isn't cynicism; it's the most reliable predictor of institutional behavior I know. It explains documentation, it explains which technologies get adopted, and it explains a great deal of what patients experience as coldness.

**Ship, then learn.** Business runs on cycles of weeks. You test, iterate, fail, and adjust, and the feedback arrives fast enough to be useful. Medicine's cycles are years long for good reasons — the cost of being wrong is a person — but the habit of expecting feedback, and of designing so that feedback is possible, is transferable even where the speed isn't.

## Hierarchy and agency

There's a structural difference underneath all four.

In medical school your world is defined by steps. Preclinical, clinical, residency, fellowship, and maybe you change something in twenty years. It isn't a bad path. It's a narrow one, and it teaches a specific relationship to time: endurance is the virtue, and initiative is something you earn the right to later.

Business broke that open for me. It reminded me I didn't need permission to start, and that I didn't have to hold a title to create value.

I want to be careful here, because the naive version of this observation is wrong and I held the naive version for a while. Chapter Three is the record of what happened when I brought startup agency into a hospital and discovered the rules were different for reasons that partly made sense. Medicine's deference isn't only sclerosis. Some of it is a system that has learned, expensively, that confident individuals acting unilaterally can hurt people.

But the culture does punish initiative and reward endurance, and that is genuinely dangerous in a field this badly in need of change. Both of those are true at once, and holding both is the harder skill.

## Users, not just patients

The last transfer is the one I think about most.

In clinical training you learn to treat a disease. In business you learn to serve a person over time — not only in the moment of the transaction but across the whole relationship, including all the parts where nothing is being sold.

That's a genuine shift in what you're optimizing. A patient encounter has a defined start and end and a documented outcome. A user relationship has none of those; it's continuous, and its quality shows up in whether people come back, whether they trust you, whether they tell you the truth.

Medicine has a version of this and calls it continuity of care, and prizes it in principle while structuring almost nothing to support it. The fifteen-minute visit is not designed for a relationship. It's designed for a transaction, and then we're surprised when patients treat it as one.

A great deal of what this book calls incentive misalignment is visible immediately if you've spent time thinking about users, because the questions are the ones any product person asks by reflex. What does this person actually need? What are they experiencing right now? What happens after they leave? Who is this designed for?

That last question is the one that keeps producing uncomfortable answers, and it's the one I'd hand to a reader as the takeaway of this chapter. When you look at a hospital workflow, an electronic record, a consent form, or a billing statement, ask who it was designed for. Not who uses it. Who it was *designed for*.

The answer is rarely the patient, and once you can see that, you can't stop seeing it.

## What this doesn't give me

I should say what the outside vantage doesn't provide, since the next chapters lean on it heavily.

It doesn't give me clinical judgment, which is real, takes years, and is not reducible to systems thinking. It doesn't give me the experience of having harmed someone, which changes how a person weighs risk in ways I can currently only theorize about. And it comes with its own distortion: people who've built things are prone to seeing every institution as a slow startup, which flattens the reasons institutions are slow.

I've tried to correct for that throughout. Chapter Seven exists mostly for that purpose, and Chapter Thirteen is the case where I think the institution is right and the technologists are wrong.

But the habits above are the instrument I'm using, and the reader should know what it is before I start pointing it at things.
