# Author's Note

A word about where this comes from, before the argument starts.

I am a medical student. At the time I wrote most of this I was two years in, just beginning rotations. My views are high on theory and low on clinical reality, and I expect them to change as I spend more time in hospitals. Anyone who tells you their opinions survived contact with the wards unchanged is either not paying attention or not telling you the truth.

That's an unusual thing to lead with in a book that spends three hundred pages arguing that a profession has something wrong. It's also the honest description of my position, and there's a reason I'd rather state it plainly than let a reader discover it in chapter three and feel misled.

Medicine has a trust problem that worries me considerably more than being wrong does.

Patients are quietly withdrawing their trust, and not because they've been misled. They're withdrawing it because too many of their questions get answered with a tone rather than a reason. I see the other end of that from the startup side every week, and the last chapters of this book are about where it leads.

Transparency is the only way to extend a hand to people who already feel dismissed by the establishment. We can't regain their trust while hiding how we think. So this book is partly an attempt to think out loud — to put my reasoning where it can be examined, invite the correction, and build a record of having argued honestly rather than only having been right.

If I'm wrong about any of this, I want to be proven wrong with evidence rather than dismissed with an appeal to authority. And if I'm right, I want the argument to be strong enough that it moves something.

Three practical notes.

**On sources.** Parts of this book began as essays I published while in training. They've been rewritten rather than collected — the arguments have been extended, the objections stated more fully, and in several places I reached conclusions that contradict what I originally wrote. Where that happened I left the contradiction visible instead of quietly updating.

**On the timestamp.** The premise of this book is that I'm writing from a narrow window before medical training finishes teaching me to see its assumptions as facts. That window is closing while you read this. Some of what follows is already less true of me than it was when I wrote it, which is a strange thing to admit in a preface and is also the entire point.

**On what I'm not claiming.** I have less clinical knowledge than nearly everyone I write about here. Nothing in this book is a claim to know medicine better than the people practicing it. It's a claim to be standing somewhere slightly different while I look at the same thing.

That's the whole of the advantage, and it expires.

— Ali

# Not Yet Captured

We had a class one day about medical tourism.

The professor opened with a simple exercise. Raise your hand if you'd get a whole-body MRI, if someone offered you one for free.

About half the class raised their hands.

What followed was a carefully constructed ninety minutes on the dangers of overdiagnosis. The incidental findings that lead to unnecessary biopsies. The anxiety of ambiguous results. The inequities of boutique medicine. As a practical example they showed us a screening company's website and had us critique how it was marketed to worried wealthy people.

At the end, the professors asked us to vote again on the same question.

I think they expected fewer hands. That we'd been educated out of our naive enthusiasm for information about our own bodies.

The room went quiet, and then broke into a handful of awkward, honest laughs, because we were all looking around at each other and there were *more* hands up than at the beginning.

The lead professor's face was a mixture of shock and disappointment. I was trying not to grin.

Both of those reactions were correct, which is the thing I've never been able to get past. The professor sees, every year, the anxiety that whole-body imaging causes real people — the cascade, the biopsy, the harm done to someone who was never sick. I saw a room full of people who had just been given the best available case against something and had quietly concluded they still wanted it. He saw naivety. I saw a signal.

That vote is why this book exists.

## The thing that happens to everyone

Here is the mechanism that interests me more than any individual argument in this book.

Almost everyone who becomes a physician enters medicine knowing nothing else. They decide at nineteen, or fifteen, or nine. They spend four years on prerequisites, four in medical school, three to seven in residency and fellowship, inside an institution with strong opinions about how things are done and excellent methods for transmitting them.

By the time they're practicing, the profession's assumptions have stopped looking like assumptions. They look like facts about how medicine has to work — and the person holding them has no other professional life to compare against, because they've never had one.

I don't mean this as an insult. It's not a failure of intelligence; the people I train with are the fastest learners I've ever been around. It's what happens to anyone immersed in a single institution long enough. It happens to lawyers, to soldiers, to academics, to me. The water stops being visible.

What's unusual about medicine is only how complete the immersion is, how early it starts, and how much of what gets absorbed is defended in the language of patient safety — which makes it very difficult to examine, because examining it sounds like arguing against safety.

I arrived from somewhere else.

## Where I arrived from

I was twelve the last time I moved countries.

By then I'd lived in Pakistan, Lebanon, Kenya, and America. Four countries before middle school. I didn't choose any of it — you don't, when you're a child — but it did something to my brain I've never been able to undo. It made it impossible for me to believe that the way things are done is the only way they could be done.

Every place had a different set of rules. Different things that were obvious. Different things that were unspeakable. And because I watched those rules change completely every few years, I could never fully commit to any of them. I was always, at some level, a visitor. Watching. Taking notes.

Then I spent years building and running a business before medical school, which taught me a second version of the same lesson: that most of what an institution treats as fixed is a choice somebody made, usually for reasons that have since expired.

So I came into medicine with a comparison set. Not a better one — just a different one, and enough of one that the profession's assumptions arrived to me as *choices* rather than as the shape of reality.

That's the entire vantage point of this book. Not expertise — the people I'm writing about know far more medicine than I do, and will for years. Just the accident of having somewhere else to stand while I look at the same thing they're looking at.

## The window is closing

I can already feel it going, and Chapter Three is the record of it.

I came in with the founder's conviction that nobody is watching you — a belief that is true and useful in business and turned out to be exactly wrong in medicine. Within two years I'd learned that everyone is watching, that I now represented an institution and a profession rather than only myself, and that the personality I'd spent years cultivating was a liability in a room built for a different rubric.

I learned that lesson correctly. It's a real lesson and I'm a better colleague for it.

It is also, precisely, the capture happening. That's what it feels like from inside: not indoctrination but a series of reasonable corrections, each one true, accumulating into a disposition. Nobody sat me down. I simply noticed, over twenty-four months, that things which had seemed strange had stopped seeming strange, and I could no longer reliably tell which of them had been resolved and which had merely become familiar.

Three more years of this and I won't be able to write this book. Not because anyone will stop me, but because I'll have lost the comparison. I'll look at the same practices and see medicine.

So this is a book with an expiration date built into its premise, written by someone who can see the date approaching. That's a strange position to write from, and I've tried to use it rather than apologize for it.

## What this book is and isn't

It isn't an exposé. I'm not going to tell you that doctors are venal or that the profession is a racket. Almost everything I criticize in these pages was built by people acting reasonably inside constraints they didn't choose, and the ones defending it are usually protecting patients with the time and tools they have.

It also isn't a memoir, though there's biography in it where the biography is the evidence.

It's an argument, and the argument is narrow enough to state on one page.

Medicine has internalized scarcity as a virtue — in testing, in data, in access, in what patients are told about their own bodies. That instinct was a good adaptation to a world where tests were expensive, invasive, and genuinely limited. That world is going away unevenly, and the instinct is outliving it. Because it's carried as identity rather than as technique, it will keep outliving it, and each new technology will spend a decade or two waiting for permission while people who could have been helped are not.

The specific error underneath is that medicine treats gathering information and taking action as the same category of risk. They aren't. Information is cheap now; intervention still isn't. Restraint belongs on the second and has quietly been extended over the first, and almost nobody has examined the extension because it never had to be examined before.

That's the whole thesis. Everything else is a case, an objection, or a consequence.

## How to read what follows

Part One is who's speaking and why you should or shouldn't believe him — including a full chapter of things I've been confidently wrong about, placed before the critique on purpose.

Part Two is the diagnosis: the incentive structure underneath the dysfunction, the scarcity reflex itself, the strongest objections to my own argument, and two reasoning tools you'll need later. One of those chapters does real damage to my thesis, and I've left the damage in place rather than repairing it.

Part Three tests the pattern against specific fights — a scanner, a specialty, a framework for when technology actually kills a profession. One of those chapters concludes that medicine is right and the technologists are wrong. That chapter is there because a critic who can never reach that conclusion isn't offering a diagnosis, only a preference with citations.

Part Four is about money and motive, which the profession discusses badly, and then what I'd actually change.

## What I'm promising

Specific and fixable, not general and aggrieved.

Every complaint in this book comes attached to a mechanism — why the thing happens, what would have to be true for it not to, and what would show me wrong. Where I don't have that, I say so. There are things in here I believe and can't prove, and they're labeled.

I'd rather be wrong in a way that's checkable than right in a way that isn't.

And I'd rather write it now, from inside the window, than in fifteen years when I'll have the standing to say it and won't be able to see it anymore.

Half the room put their hands up the second time. My professor thought that was a failure of teaching.

I think they were telling him something, and I've spent this book trying to work out what.

# What Business Taught Me That Medical School Never Could

Medical school teaches you how to identify a problem. Business taught me how to solve one.

That's the shortest way I can put the difference, and like most short formulations it's unfair to both. Medicine solves problems constantly and at a level of precision business never approaches — no startup has ever had to be right about a potassium level. But the *kind* of problem each discipline trains you to see is genuinely different, and the gap between them is the whole reason this book exists.

This chapter is about what specifically transfers. Not attitude, not confidence, not the vague entrepreneurial energy people mean when they say a doctor is "business-minded." A set of concrete analytical habits that medical training doesn't install, that I use on every argument in the rest of this book, and that the reader will need in order to follow what I'm doing.

## Individuals inside systems

In medical school you're trained to gather information, analyze data, recognize patterns, and intervene with precision. It's a beautiful craft and I don't want to be glib about it.

But if you stop there, you end up treating individuals inside broken systems — systems nobody taught you to see, let alone repair. You become extremely good at the part of the problem that fits inside a room.

Business filled in that gap for me. It taught me to think in systems, to design around friction, to align incentives, and to notice that "how things move" is itself a subject you can study. It also taught me something that sounds obvious and isn't: you don't need to be a policy expert or a billionaire to change how something works. You need to understand the mechanism, and then build something that moves it better.

## The four habits

Here is what actually transfers, stated as precisely as I can manage.

**Read the incentive before the stated reason.** In business you learn quickly that what people say they want and what the structure rewards them for are different things, and that when the two conflict the structure wins. Not because people are dishonest — because incentives operate below the level of stated intention. This is the single most useful analytical habit I have, and it's the engine of Chapter Five. When someone tells you a policy exists for patient safety, that may be entirely true. It is also a claim you can check, by asking who benefits from the policy and what would change if it disappeared.

**Treat "that's how it's done" as a claim rather than a fact.** Every organization carries practices whose original justification expired quietly. In a startup you learn to ask why constantly, partly because there's no institutional memory to defer to and partly because inherited practice is usually where the waste is hiding. Medicine has enormous institutional memory, which makes this question feel impertinent rather than routine. It's the same question.

**Follow the money to the behavior.** If you want to know why a workflow looks the way it does, look at what gets reimbursed. This isn't cynicism; it's the most reliable predictor of institutional behavior I know. It explains documentation, it explains which technologies get adopted, and it explains a great deal of what patients experience as coldness.

**Ship, then learn.** Business runs on cycles of weeks. You test, iterate, fail, and adjust, and the feedback arrives fast enough to be useful. Medicine's cycles are years long for good reasons — the cost of being wrong is a person — but the habit of expecting feedback, and of designing so that feedback is possible, is transferable even where the speed isn't.

## Hierarchy and agency

There's a structural difference underneath all four.

In medical school your world is defined by steps. Preclinical, clinical, residency, fellowship, and maybe you change something in twenty years. It isn't a bad path. It's a narrow one, and it teaches a specific relationship to time: endurance is the virtue, and initiative is something you earn the right to later.

Business broke that open for me. It reminded me I didn't need permission to start, and that I didn't have to hold a title to create value.

I want to be careful here, because the naive version of this observation is wrong and I held the naive version for a while. Chapter Three is the record of what happened when I brought startup agency into a hospital and discovered the rules were different for reasons that partly made sense. Medicine's deference isn't only sclerosis. Some of it is a system that has learned, expensively, that confident individuals acting unilaterally can hurt people.

But the culture does punish initiative and reward endurance, and that is genuinely dangerous in a field this badly in need of change. Both of those are true at once, and holding both is the harder skill.

## Users, not just patients

The last transfer is the one I think about most.

In clinical training you learn to treat a disease. In business you learn to serve a person over time — not only in the moment of the transaction but across the whole relationship, including all the parts where nothing is being sold.

That's a genuine shift in what you're optimizing. A patient encounter has a defined start and end and a documented outcome. A user relationship has none of those; it's continuous, and its quality shows up in whether people come back, whether they trust you, whether they tell you the truth.

Medicine has a version of this and calls it continuity of care, and prizes it in principle while structuring almost nothing to support it. The fifteen-minute visit is not designed for a relationship. It's designed for a transaction, and then we're surprised when patients treat it as one.

A great deal of what this book calls incentive misalignment is visible immediately if you've spent time thinking about users, because the questions are the ones any product person asks by reflex. What does this person actually need? What are they experiencing right now? What happens after they leave? Who is this designed for?

That last question is the one that keeps producing uncomfortable answers, and it's the one I'd hand to a reader as the takeaway of this chapter. When you look at a hospital workflow, an electronic record, a consent form, or a billing statement, ask who it was designed for. Not who uses it. Who it was *designed for*.

The answer is rarely the patient, and once you can see that, you can't stop seeing it.

## What this doesn't give me

I should say what the outside vantage doesn't provide, since the next chapters lean on it heavily.

It doesn't give me clinical judgment, which is real, takes years, and is not reducible to systems thinking. It doesn't give me the experience of having harmed someone, which changes how a person weighs risk in ways I can currently only theorize about. And it comes with its own distortion: people who've built things are prone to seeing every institution as a slow startup, which flattens the reasons institutions are slow.

I've tried to correct for that throughout. Chapter Seven exists mostly for that purpose, and Chapter Thirteen is the case where I think the institution is right and the technologists are wrong.

But the habits above are the instrument I'm using, and the reader should know what it is before I start pointing it at things.

# What I Got Wrong

I'm halfway through medical school. Two years in, rotations just started, psychiatry first.

When I got in, I felt something I hadn't expected: lost.

Not because I didn't want to be there. Medicine had been the dream since I was a child — I never weighed it against alternatives, I simply always knew. I liked science, I liked helping people, and in every story I knew the doctor was the one who showed up when it mattered.

But something strange happens when you finally reach the thing you've been chasing your whole life. You arrive, you pause, and then you ask: now what? People celebrate the acceptance letter and the white coat. Nobody prepares you for the dream coming true and leaving you incomplete.

I think that feeling is the earliest evidence for everything in this book. Someone who feels lost on arriving was already, without knowing it, measuring medicine against something else.

Before I spend the rest of this book explaining what my profession has gotten wrong, I want to go through what I got wrong, because I think the exercise is worth more than it looks. Most people quietly bury their incorrect predictions. I think that's foolish. What you were wrong about is where the information is — the place where your model of the world and the world itself came apart, and therefore the only place you can tune the model.

There's also a simpler reason to put this chapter before the critique. If I'm going to ask a profession to audit its own delays and stop describing them as prudence afterward, I should demonstrate that I can do it at the scale of one person first.

## I thought the path had to be fifty years long

The biggest thing I got wrong was written down, in college, as a plan.

Become a doctor. Build patient trust. Use that to influence policy. Move into politics. Then pharma, to build capital. Use the capital to fund schools and hospitals. Then return to medicine, finally free of the constraints that made the whole detour necessary.

Fifty years. My friends knew it by name.

Looking back, it's the kind of ambitious, hyper-structured plan only an analytical college student writes. It assumed the world was a puzzle — that if I played every move correctly across five decades, I would eventually *earn* the right to fix things. Every step was a permission slip for the next one.

What broke it was a year I hadn't planned for. I graduated, joined Epic, and resigned to go to medical school — but the gap between those two things turned into a year of building on my own, and that year taught me the plan had a false premise buried in step one.

I didn't need to wait fifty years. I didn't need permission to start solving problems. I could start immediately, badly, at small scale, and learn more in eight months than the first decade of the plan would have taught me.

So I scrapped it. Not adjusted it — scrapped it. I had been on a trajectory to spend forty years earning access to problems I could already work on.

I want to be precise about what was wrong, because "just start" is the sort of advice that sounds good and means nothing. The plan wasn't wrong about the destination. It was wrong about the *mechanism* — it treated influence as something conferred by accumulated standing rather than something produced by having done useful work. Those look identical from inside a college library. They are not the same thing, and only one of them can be started on a Tuesday.

*Lesson: when a plan requires you to wait for permission at every stage, check whether the permission is real or whether you invented it to justify the waiting.*

## I thought I could run the agency through medical school

I built a services business because I had more time than money, so I sold time to people who had more money than time. That was the correct read of my situation.

What I failed to notice was that medical school changes the equation entirely. Now I had less time *and* less money. The whole basis of the arbitrage disappeared, and I kept operating as though it hadn't for longer than I should have.

*Lesson: always be clear about what you have that the other person doesn't. And check whether you still have it.*

## I thought medical school wouldn't actually be that hard

As a premed I used to say that premeds love to complain. It looked to me like a group that bonded over shared misery. I was taking engineering, computer science, business, and international relations courses alongside, and the people in those rooms were working just as hard — often harder, especially the engineers — without making it their identity.

That left me with a sour view: medicine attracts people who think they're doing the hardest thing in the world. At the premed stage I still think that's roughly accurate.

I assumed medical school would be more of the same. I was wrong, and not slightly.

Medical school is genuinely, brutally hard. The people in my class and in classes across the country are among the hardest workers I have encountered anywhere, with an ability to absorb and retain volume that I can only describe as superhuman. In raw learning horsepower these are some of the most capable people alive, and I had spent years assuming they were complainers.

*Lesson: becoming genuinely knowledgeable in anything is an enormous amount of work. There is no version where it isn't.*

## I romanticized what medicine was

I grew up believing medicine was the summit of higher education. I pictured myself among Osler and Avicenna and Sydenham — a discipline of scientific rigor and exactness, of the humble acceptance that we know nothing for certain, held alongside the obligation to act decisively anyway to protect the vulnerable.

In many ways it is exactly that. What surprised me is how much the broader currents of society now shape daily practice and training. Physicians are increasingly expected to address not only disease but the full range of social and environmental factors bearing on health. I'm not saying that's good or bad; it reflects the world we live in. But it wasn't what I expected. I had imagined a sharper focus on pathophysiology, diagnostics, and therapeutics as the core of professional training.

*Lesson: it is usually foolish to expect the cultural zeitgeist to leave any part of society untouched. Assume it penetrates everything.*

## I brought the wrong mindset from entrepreneurship

This is the one that matters most for the rest of this book.

After a couple of years running my own businesses, I had absorbed the mentality that makes zero-to-one possible: nobody cares about you as much as you think. People are not watching, judging, or thinking about you nearly as much as your fear insists. The anxiety that stops you from putting yourself out there is, at bottom, a kind of selfish overestimation of your role in other people's lives. Nobody's paying attention. So go do the thing.

In business that reasoning is true and it helped me enormously.

I carried it into medical school, where it turned out to be exactly wrong.

I stood out like a peacock. Like a tall poppy. Like the guy who couldn't stop talking about everything else he was doing. I drew attention, some of it good and some of it not, because the rubric wasn't built for the personality I'd spent two years cultivating. I earned genuine respect from many people for the things I've always been known for — bluntness, sincerity, willingness to say the thing that needed saying and to speak for perspectives that weren't in the room.

But suddenly everything was being observed. Suddenly everyone *did* care. Suddenly I no longer represented only myself; I represented an institution, a profession. I had gone from being an underdog with nothing to lose to something closer to the player who doesn't understand that the team is bigger than he is. I didn't see it early. I see it now, and I walk that line much more carefully.

I want to name what that experience actually is, because it's the subject of this entire book happening to its author.

That is the capture mechanism. Not a conspiracy, not an initiation — just the ordinary, continuous pressure of an institution teaching a person that his visibility is now a liability held in common. It works. It worked on me within two years, and I was arriving with an unusually strong prior against it and a documented history of writing publicly. The people around me who came in straight from undergrad, with no other professional identity to compare it to, absorbed it faster and with less friction, because for them there was nothing to convert *from*.

This is why Chapter One insists the window is narrow. I can feel it closing. Some of what still looks strange to me will look normal in three years, and I will not be able to tell which parts.

*Lesson: always know what game you're playing and what the rules are. If you're playing soccer while everyone else plays lacrosse, you'll lose badly, look foolish doing it, and your team will suffer. Also know whether you're in an individual sport or a team sport. Medicine is a team sport.*

## My guilty secret

Before medical school I carried something I was faintly ashamed of. I believed most of what medicine does is less powerful than the fundamentals — sunlight, exercise, real food, sleep, close relationships. I thought those alone could carry most people to nearly the healthiest life their genes allowed, preventing or treating an enormous share of illness and avoiding a lot of unnecessary harm.

I took the Hippocratic oath holding that quietly.

I still believe the fundamentals matter enormously. I've also seen too much to stay that simplistic.

I've watched medications and interventions change trajectories in severe cases where lifestyle measures were never going to be enough. My earlier view underestimated both the complexity of human biology and the reality of suffering that requires targeted help. Psychiatry in particular has struck me — I've been impressed by how carefully my attendings and residents work to use the minimum effective dose and to reach for non-pharmacologic tools first. The patients who stay on medication long-term are generally those for whom stopping carries clearly greater risk.

*Lesson: hold convictions, stay open to being wrong. People are usually trying to do the right thing with the tools and information they have. Not everyone has the same tools, and that doesn't make them malicious — it usually just reflects the limits of their environment and training.*

That last sentence is the one I most want the reader to carry into Part Two. Nearly everyone I am about to disagree with is doing their best inside a structure they did not design and cannot individually change.

## I thought medical school would give me an unfair advantage

I assumed that being inside would be a massive competitive advantage. I'd have access to hospital decision-makers, and as someone with a background in software, marketing, and design, I had what looked like the perfect toolkit: find problems, build solutions, get them in front of the people who could deploy them.

I was delusional about this.

Healthcare may be the slowest sales cycle I will ever encounter. Despite being on good terms with senior people at various hospitals, there are endless steps and gatekeepers regardless of who you know or where you enter.

I built a system I was certain would win. The idea was a spell check for billing.

Hospitals submit claims, insurers deny a fraction of them, and the denials are expensive in a way that compounds: staff time to identify the problem, staff time to rework it, delay in payment, and sometimes revenue simply written off. Most of those denials are not fraud or dispute. They are coding errors — a claim assembled slightly wrong, submitted, and rejected for a reason that would have been obvious if anyone had looked before it went out.

So: catch them before submission. A model trained on that specific hospital's own historical claims, learning which patterns had been denied before, flagging a claim on the way out the door the way a word processor flags a misspelling. Not a rules engine bought off a shelf and applied uniformly, which is what already existed and which is why it worked poorly. Something trained on the institution's own history, because denial patterns are local — they depend on which payers you contract with and which of their idiosyncrasies you keep tripping over.

I built the MVP in a weekend, right after an email went out to the hospital community about hiring a consulting agency to address revenue cycle management problems. The timing seemed perfect.

I walked to the CEO's office. He directed me to the Senior VP of Billing. The SVP expressed some interest. Then he never quite responded again.

That was the lesson, and it cost me nothing but a weekend and some pride, which is a cheap price for it. Hospitals don't want to buy systems that are new. They want to buy systems that let them sleep at night because someone else already de-risked them. Providing that is much harder than building the thing, and it requires a kind of creativity I hadn't developed.

I want to flag something about this story, because it's easy to read as a complaint about institutional sclerosis and it isn't only that. Every chapter in Part Three describes institutional lag from the outside — the decade and a half, the profession failing to move. This is what it looked like from inside, aimed at me, and my read at the time was that the institution was being obtuse. Some of it was. Some of it was a large organization correctly declining to run unvalidated software against its revenue cycle on the word of a second-year student who had built it over a weekend.

Both readings are true. Holding both is most of what this book is trying to do.

*Lesson: proximity, credentials, and timing all matter. Persistence matters more. You don't need to be an insider if you're a relentless outsider — and if you think being an insider is itself the advantage, you probably won't do the things the relentless outsider is doing, and they'll win. Being inside is an add-on. It is not a substitute for any of the work.*

## Why this chapter is here

I could have opened this book with the argument. Putting this first costs me something — the reader now knows I've been confidently wrong about my own profession, repeatedly, in the two years I've been inside it.

I think that's the correct trade. The alternative is a book that asks a century-old profession to hold its beliefs loosely, written by someone who won't.

Everything after this is an argument I believe. Some of it will turn out to be wrong in ways I can't currently see, which is exactly what the last four years have taught me to expect. The list above is the evidence that when that happens, I'll say so.

# Turn the Volume Up, Doctor

There's a moment in an old podcast episode that captures the fault line between the entrepreneurial world and the medical establishment better than anything I could construct.

One of the hosts was describing a period of founder paralysis — the specific inertia where you have the capital and the energy but can't decide what to build next. He mentioned it to his trainer, who stopped him mid-sentence.

*You are the product. The product is just you pushed out. So do you, but turn the volume knob up.*

If you're a founder, an engineer, or a marketer, that sentence lands like a green light you were already looking for. It means stripping away corporate abstraction and relying on something more direct.

If you're a physician, the same sentence sounds like a structural threat. It triggers something close to an allergic reaction.

I've thought about why for a long time, and this chapter is the answer. It's also, unavoidably, the book's own permission slip — the argument that justifies its existence, which means the reader should watch me carefully here, because I have an obvious interest in reaching a particular conclusion.

## The institutional cult of anonymity

Medicine trains invisibility on purpose, and the conditioning starts on day one.

Consider Joel Bervell, a medical student who began producing short videos on clinical blind spots — racial bias in pulse oximetry, in lung function algorithms. Objectively vital public health information, exactly the kind of evidence translation the public needs. His institutional advisors warned him to think twice before speaking publicly about hot-button issues. [^1]

The warning wasn't about accuracy. Nobody disputed the data, and the data was not marginal. Sjoding and colleagues, examining nearly fifty thousand paired measurements, found Black patients had close to three times the rate of occult hypoxemia missed by pulse oximetry as white patients. Among readings showing an oxygen saturation of 92 to 96 percent, actual arterial saturation was below 88 percent in twelve percent of measurements in Black patients and four percent in white patients. [^2]

A device in every hospital room in the country was systematically less accurate for some patients than for others. The objection was never that he had the science wrong. It was that he was visible at all — and in the legacy paradigm, visibility itself is treated as a risk-management failure.

And the conditioning starts long before it reaches the internet. We are trained toward a sterile emotional distance from patients under the banner of objective professionalism. The system wants the physician to be an interchangeable node — a white coat, a stethoscope, an institutional logo. No edge, no distinct perspective, no volume.

## What silence actually costs

This isn't merely outdated. It's actively harmful, and the mechanism is simple.

When medicine codes silence as a virtue, it doesn't stop people from seeking medical information. It only ensures they won't get it from us. Treating public visibility as a professionalism violation created an enormous information vacuum.

Vacuums fill.

Because actual physicians are afraid of institutional friction, the public square went to biohackers, wellness influencers, and people selling unvetted supplements. They have no clinical training and complete mastery of the volume knob. That's the whole trade: we kept our professional decorum and handed over the audience.

When a licensed physician decides that staying invisible is the safe career move, they are not protecting patients. They're outsourcing the public's health education to the loudest, least qualified voices available.

Invisibility is not a neutral ethical stance. It's a dereliction.

## The second reason for silence, which is newer

There's a force pushing the same direction that almost nobody in medicine is discussing, and it will get worse.

When we built the internet, the premise was that information would spread further and faster than ever. Publishing knowledge was a straightforward win: you gained reputation, built an audience, helped people, and staked a kind of intellectual claim.

AI is quietly inverting that incentive. If you develop something genuinely new — an idea, a technique, a way of doing something — there's a growing chance that publishing it doesn't make you known. It makes you redundant. You've handed it to a system that never sleeps, never forgets, and can deliver your insight to anyone who asks, instantly, without attribution.

I don't think this changes what physicians should do. But I think it explains something about why the vacuum will keep widening, and it means the argument for speaking has to be made on different grounds than it was ten years ago. The reputational return on sharing expertise is falling. The public need for it isn't.

So the case for visibility can't rest on what it does for your career. It has to rest on what happens if nobody does it.

## A workable definition of professionalism

The current standard is incoherent. Write a peer-reviewed paper three people read and you're a scientist. Summarize that same paper in plain English for a hundred thousand people and you're a self-promoter.

A better test comes from a thought experiment I've found genuinely clarifying: how would you behave if there were zero possibility of punishment or reward?

Authenticity, on that definition, is the collapse of the gap between public and private persona. There's no difference between who you are when the microphones are off and who you are when they're live.

That gives a physician two usable filters instead of vague institutional hand-wringing.

**The clinical alignment test.** Would I say this exact thing to a patient sitting across from me, using my genuine clinical judgment? If yes, then staying quiet online isn't professional discipline. It's fear wearing the costume of ethics.

**The performance filter.** Am I saying this only because it performs for an algorithm, or projects a confidence I don't actually have? If yes, *that's* the real professional violation — and notice it has nothing to do with volume. It's about deceit.

Those two tests do the work the profession's current norms pretend to do, and they have the advantage of being answerable.

## Blue links

There's a story about the early web I keep returning to.

When Marc Andreessen was asked why hyperlinks are blue, the answer was that he liked blue. No committee, no study, no consensus. One person's preference, stamped onto the architecture of the internet, and now billions of people live inside a world colored by it.

The story is trivial on the surface and not underneath. It reveals the actual scaffolding of culture: it isn't built by invisible forces or natural law. It's built by people who asserted that their preference should become reality.

Most people, faced with that, flinch — because admitting the world is shaped by people who stepped forward means admitting you could have. It's easier to believe in inevitability, in systems and institutions and the way things are, and to call the resulting passivity humility or realism.

Medicine is unusually good at manufacturing that flinch. It teaches deference as a professional virtue, which is correct in a clinical hierarchy where a confident junior can kill someone, and then extends it into every domain including ones where deference costs lives rather than saving them.

Every guideline, every standard, every norm this profession operates under was once somebody's argument. They're not weather.

## What I'm doing here

I should be direct about the self-interest, because a chapter arguing that physicians should speak publicly, written by a physician-in-training publishing a book, is not a neutral document.

I write because the intersection of technology, medicine, and systems thinking is where my mind goes when nobody is paying me to think about anything. I would write it with zero readers. That's the only test I trust for whether the work is real, and it's the one I'd apply to anyone making this argument, including me.

The credentials on a wall were never meant to be a shield to hide behind. They were meant to be a foundation to build on.

The physicians actually changing the landscape aren't inventing a persona. They're taking the clinical judgment, clear communication, and lack of jargon they already use to help people, and pushing it outward.

That's the whole ask. Stop letting institutional inertia set your reach. The product is your judgment.

Turn the volume up.

# Why Healthcare Feels Broken

In my first few months of medical school I kept hearing the same sentence, from attendings, from nurses, from administrators, delivered in the tone people use for weather.

*The healthcare system is broken.*

At first I took it for burnout talking. The longer I spent inside medicine while also working in technology and running a business, the more I came to think it was something else. Healthcare isn't randomly dysfunctional. It's *predictably* dysfunctional, structured in ways that reliably produce outcomes nobody involved would choose.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. Random dysfunction is bad luck and you address it with effort. Predictable dysfunction is design, and design can be read — which means it can also be changed by whoever is willing to look at the blueprint instead of the weather.

The reason healthcare feels broken is simpler than most people expect. The incentives are systematically misaligned, and everyone is behaving rationally inside them.

## Three reasonable goals that don't fit together

When people say healthcare is broken they usually mean that patients, physicians, and institutions are all frustrated and all behaving in ways that look irrational from outside. But the frustrations aren't accidental. They're symptoms of a structural problem you can state in three lines.

Patients want affordable, high-quality care that improves their long-term health.

Physicians want autonomy, meaning, efficiency, and the ability to actually help the person in front of them.

Hospitals and health systems want stable revenue, manageable risk, and compliance with the metrics they're measured against.

None of these is unreasonable. Not one of the three is held by villains. The problem is that the incentives guiding day-to-day behavior almost never align all three at once, and incentives shape behavior whether or not anyone notices them doing it. When incentives are misaligned, well-intentioned people produce outcomes that are suboptimal or plainly irrational, and each of them can explain exactly why their own part was necessary.

This isn't unique to healthcare. Misaligned incentives produce dysfunction everywhere — in business, in government, in universities. Healthcare is distinctive only in that the misalignment lands directly on human wellbeing rather than on a quarterly number.

## The clearest example: documentation

Take something every physician experiences daily.

Doctors spend enormous amounts of time documenting patient encounters. On its face this is obviously good — detailed records support safety, continuity, and accountability. Nobody argues for worse charts.

But look at what the documentation is actually optimized for. Hospitals and clinics require highly detailed notes primarily because that's how billing works. The more thorough the documentation, the more defensible the codes, and the more reliable the reimbursement. That's not corruption. It's the system responding correctly to how it gets paid.

Physicians, meanwhile, aren't paid for documenting. They're paid for encounters. Every hour spent on notes is an hour not spent on care, which erodes the thing that drew most of them into medicine in the first place. So the same activity is a revenue requirement for the institution and a pure cost for the clinician performing it.

Patients experience the result as rushed visits and a doctor who is looking at a screen. They don't know they're watching billing code optimization. They just know the person across from them isn't quite present.

One activity. Three participants. Three incompatible relationships to it. Nobody did anything wrong, and the outcome is worse for everyone.

Multiply that by every workflow in a hospital and you have the system.

## Why this chapter comes before the rest

Everything that follows in this book is an instance of the pattern above.

The scarcity reflex in Chapter Six is a misalignment between what a clinician is rewarded for demonstrating and what a patient needs to know. The adoption lag in Chapter Twelve is a misalignment between how fast a technology can improve and how fast an institution can absorb liability. The silence in Chapter Four is a misalignment between what a profession's public voice is worth to society and what it costs the individual physician who uses it.

Once you're looking for incentive misalignment you find it everywhere, and the finding is useful rather than merely cynical, because misalignment is a design property. It was built. It can be rebuilt.

## Four realignments

I'll go into what I'd actually change at the end of this book. Four are worth naming here because they're the load-bearing ones and every later chapter touches at least one.

**Pay for outcomes rather than volume.** Fee-for-service pays for procedures and visits, so it produces procedures and visits. Value-based models — paying for health rather than for activity — align the incentive far more closely with what patients want. I hold this less confidently than I did a year ago, because the implementations so far have generated a great deal of administrative overhead and less benefit than promised. The direction is right. The execution has been poor enough that I want to be honest about not knowing whether it scales.

**Protect clinical judgment.** A great deal of frustration comes from highly trained people stripped of autonomy by processes designed for compliance and billing. Protocols have their place; medicine got safer when it stopped relying purely on individual brilliance. But when physicians lose judgment entirely, burnout rises and care suffers, and the system loses the thing it spent a decade training into them.

**Build technology for care rather than for billing.** Electronic records optimize for reimbursement and compliance because that's who bought them. That is a design choice, not a technical constraint. Software can dramatically improve how information moves and how decisions get made — I've watched it happen in industries with far less at stake. The tools we have were built for the wrong customer.

**Make price and outcomes visible.** Healthcare is one of the only major sectors where the person receiving the service has almost no visibility into cost or quality until afterward. Transparency would put real pressure on providers to compete on value, and would let patients make decisions that are currently impossible to make.

## Why I still chose this

Given all of it, why medicine?

Because the leverage is enormous precisely because the dysfunction is. Anything that genuinely improves how this system works produces human impact at a scale almost nothing else offers. That's the opportunity hiding inside the frustration.

My dual vantage point — building things and training clinically — clarifies one thing above all. Medicine itself isn't broken. The incentives and structures *around* medicine are, and those were designed by people. Which means they are changeable by people, and specifically by people who understand both the clinical reality and the mechanism producing it.

That is not optimism. It's just the observation that a designed system has a designer, and designers can be replaced.

## What comes next

Misalignment isn't inevitable. It's a problem of design rather than destiny, and recognizing it clearly is the first step toward fixing it in a way that lasts.

But naming the general problem is the easy part, and a book that stopped here would be one more contribution to the genre of healthcare complaint. The harder question is what specifically got designed wrong, and where the misalignment has hardened into something the profession now experiences as a virtue rather than a constraint.

That's the next chapter, and it's the one this whole book was written for.

# Scarcity as Virtue

There is a peculiar sensibility in medicine that took me most of a year to name. A friend and I started calling it an anti-abundance mentality, which is clumsy but close enough. It is the reflex that greets every new source of information — a continuous monitor, a whole-body scan, a patient portal, a wearable that logs something nobody asked it to log — with suspicion rather than curiosity. The default assumption is that more information is dangerous until proven otherwise.

To people who work in technology this looks backwards to the point of parody. In tech, data abundance is obviously good. More signal means better models, faster iteration, clearer patterns. Storage costs approach zero, so you capture everything and figure out later what mattered. The idea that you would deliberately collect *less* information about a system you are responsible for keeping alive strikes a software engineer as something between negligence and superstition.

Medicine has internalized scarcity as a virtue. And here is the part that took me longest to accept: it did so for reasons that are not stupid.

I want to be careful, because the easy version of this chapter writes itself and it is wrong. The easy version says doctors are technophobes, the establishment protects itself, and if everyone would just embrace data things would improve. That version is satisfying, and it will not survive five minutes with anyone who has actually watched a patient get hurt by a test they did not need.

The real argument is narrower and, I think, harder to dismiss. Medicine is applying one category of reasoning to two categories of thing. It treats gathering information and taking action as the same kind of risk, requiring the same kind of justification, subject to the same standing presumption against excess. They are not the same kind of risk, and the conflation of them is where most of the damage gets done.

## Why doctors learned to say no

Medical training is an extended exercise in restraint. You learn early that every intervention carries risk — radiation exposure, false positives that trigger cascading procedures, the anxiety of knowing something ambiguous about your body. Resources are genuinely finite in ways they aren't in tech: operating room time, specialist availability, someone's actual kidney. A software team that over-provisions servers wastes money. A clinical team that over-provisions interventions wastes people.

More importantly, you are taught that ordering lots of tests is the mark of a bad doctor. It suggests you don't know what you're looking for. The impressive attending is the one who needs fewer data points to reach the right diagnosis. Clinical acumen shows up as parsimony.

This makes sense in context. Shotgunning labs because you're intellectually lazy *is* bad medicine. The doctor who orders a comprehensive metabolic panel, complete blood count, lipid panel, thyroid function, vitamin levels, and tumor markers for a patient with a headache isn't being thorough — they're avoiding the harder work of clinical reasoning. The test does not think for you. Ordering all of them is a way of outsourcing a decision you have not made, and the outsourcing is visible to anyone senior enough to read the chart.

So restraint becomes sophistication. Scarcity becomes a North Star.

I want to sit with that longer than the argument strictly requires, because it is the load-bearing objection to everything that follows. The parsimony reflex is not decoration. It does real work. It is, among other things, the mechanism by which medicine polices its own laziness, and nobody has proposed a better one. A profession that could not tell the difference between the clinician who reasons and the clinician who orders would be a worse profession, and patients would be the ones paying the difference. Any argument for abundance that cannot say what replaces that function is not an argument. It is a complaint.

## When the constraint disappears

The problem is that the mentality persists after the constraint that produced it has vanished.

This is not unique to medicine. Every institution carries rules whose original justification expired quietly, without anyone noticing, because the rule had become identity rather than technique. What makes medicine's version consequential is that the expired rule now governs what people are permitted to know about their own bodies.

Consider what actually happened with continuous glucose monitoring. The FDA approved the first CGM — MiniMed's retrospective system — in 1999. Enthusiasm cooled fast, even inside the research community. The sensors drifted. Approval was for supplemental use alongside standard home glucose monitoring, not as a basis for treatment decisions. [^3]

Read the objections from that period and you find they are not primarily technical. Physicians argued that patients did not need continuous data when a quarterly A1C was sufficient. The listed barriers were lack of FDA approval for insulin dosing, cost and variable reimbursement, the need for recalibration, and lack of physician training in interpreting results. Physicians themselves were named as a major barrier to implementation, facing time demands impossible to meet in a brief visit, unclear reimbursement, medico-legal exposure, and the ordinary uncertainty that attends anything new. [^4]

That is a complete inventory of reasonable-sounding reasons to wait. Every one of them is about the system's capacity to absorb the information rather than about whether the information was true or useful. Notice that distinction, because it recurs. Almost none of the resistance to any of these technologies has been a claim that the data is wrong. It has been a claim that the data is inconvenient, and the two get argued in the same tone.

Then the evidence arrived. Patients could see patterns in real time, adjust behavior, catch dangerous swings before they became events. On 20 December 2016 — seventeen years after that first approval — the FDA expanded the Dexcom G5's indication so that continuous readings could be used to make treatment decisions without a confirmatory fingerstick, in patients aged two and older. [^5] "Too much data" had become the basis for dosing insulin.

Seventeen years.

Pulse oximetry tells a shorter version of the same story, and I want to tell it accurately rather than dramatically, because when I first wrote about it I got the timeline badly wrong.

Takuo Aoyagi worked out the principle in 1974 and presented it in Osaka that year. Nihon Kohden brought the first ear oximeter to market in 1975. It was not a commercial success, and Aoyagi's insight was largely ignored for a period. Minolta adapted and improved the design around 1978, which prompted other firms to develop and market oximeters through the mid-1980s. The American Society of Anesthesiologists made pulse oximetry a standard in 1986, and in 1988 Thomas Neff proposed treating oxygen saturation as a fifth vital sign. [^6]

So roughly twelve years from principle to standard of care in the operating room — considerably faster than CGM, and worth conceding plainly. **An earlier version of this chapter said forty years, which was wrong.** I had stretched the timeline by folding in a separate and later argument about whether routine oximetry was warranted for emergency department patients, a debate that ran into the late 1990s. Those are two different questions and merging them made the lag look worse than it was.

What survives is the smaller and more interesting fact: the first device failed commercially, and the principle sat underused for years, not because anyone showed it was wrong but because the field had not decided it wanted continuous oxygen data. The lag was in appetite rather than evidence. That is the same shape as CGM, just on a kinder clock.

Liquid biopsy is the same story happening in the present tense, where we can watch it rather than reconstruct it. Adoption for excluding patients from targeted therapy has been slow, driven largely by concern about false negatives; current guidance is that a negative liquid biopsy should be followed by tissue sequencing. [^7] The stated barriers are workflow integration, the need for clinician training in interpretation and limitations, and a lack of protocol standardization. [^8] The objections are the familiar set: false positives leading to unnecessary treatment and psychological distress, false negatives delaying diagnosis, an unsettled regulatory picture, and the management of incidental findings.

Meanwhile the evidence accumulates anyway, the way it did before. PADA-1 demonstrated improved progression-free survival using liquid biopsy to guide therapy. plasmaMATCH found ninety-six to ninety-nine percent concordance between liquid biopsy and tissue sequencing. [^9] Multi-cancer early detection tests are moving from curiosity to product while the profession is still deciding how it feels.

Three technologies, three decades apart, one sequence. A technology becomes feasible. The people who built it are enthusiastic. The establishment resists, citing practical concerns, absent evidence, false positives, cost, and workflow disruption. Contrarians use it anyway and study it. Evidence accumulates over ten to twenty years. The establishment accepts it, grudgingly. And then — this is the step that matters most — the delay gets described retroactively as appropriate caution.

That last step is what guarantees the cycle repeats. It converts a fifteen-year institutional failure into a story about prudence, and a profession that tells itself that story has learned nothing it can carry to the next technology. The lag runs somewhere between a decade and two decades from proof of concept to mainstream acceptance, varying with how badly the field wanted the thing. That number does not track how long the technology needed to mature. It tracks how long the institution needed to stop objecting.

## Four reasons the reflex outlives its reason

If the pattern is this legible, why does it keep happening? I can identify four mechanisms, and they compound.

The first is training trauma. Medical education rewards doing less and punishes shotgunning tests, which is correct as pedagogy and disastrous as permanent disposition. After years of being graded on restraint, abundance does not feel like a neutral option with tradeoffs. It feels like a professional failure — like being caught not knowing. This is the difference between a technique and an identity, and the distinction is not academic: you can update a technique when the evidence changes. Updating an identity costs something, and people are reluctant to pay it in front of colleagues.

The second is legitimate bad experience. Every doctor who has practiced long enough has seen the patient whose incidental finding led to a biopsy, which led to a complication, which led to an outcome worse than the disease that was never there. These stories have weight, and they should. But the precautionary principle gets overapplied in a specific and asymmetric way: because abundance *can* cause harm, the default becomes scarcity. The harm from information is vivid and attributable. The harm from its absence is invisible and unattributed. Nobody tells the story of the cancer that would have been caught, because nobody knows it was there.

The third is payment models frozen at old economics. Reimbursement still treats testing as expensive even as the underlying cost collapses. Insurance will pay for a comprehensive metabolic panel and not for continuous metabolic monitoring, though the second might prevent the emergency the first missed. The system's architecture assumes a scarcity the economics no longer support, and culture follows architecture far more reliably than it follows argument. This is the least glamorous item on the list and probably the most powerful.

The fourth is status. Clinical judgment is demonstrated by needing less data to reach the answer, so any tool that makes diagnosis easier threatens the demonstration. The physician who catches early sepsis from experience and intuition is impressive in a way the physician who catches it from an alert is not, even when the second one catches it earlier and the patient does better. I do not think this is usually conscious. I think it is a selection pressure applied gently and constantly to how clinicians describe their own competence, and therefore to which tools they are eager to adopt.

None of these four is stupid. Three of them are load-bearing. That is exactly what makes the reflex durable, and why "be more open to technology" is a sentiment rather than a strategy.

## The distinction the whole argument rests on

Here is the claim this chapter exists to make.

Abundance of data is usually good. Information is cheap to gather, cheap to store, cheap to analyze. Continuous monitoring, comprehensive panels, patient-generated health data — these should default to yes when the technology allows it and the patient wants it.

Abundance of action is often bad. Interventions carry real risk and real cost. You should not do procedures that will not change management. Selective decision-making is correct here, and the restraint medicine has built around it is a genuine achievement rather than a bug to be engineered away.

Any business owner understands this instinctively in their own domain. You have a hundred ideas. Each change carries a starting cost and training friction that scale across the whole operation, so you want to be confident the gains will exceed the temporary loss across the team. That is discipline about *action*. It is not an argument for knowing less about your business. Nobody has ever improved an operation by deliberately reducing its instrumentation, and if a consultant proposed it you would stop taking their calls.

The error is applying intervention-logic to information-gathering. They are different categories and they need different heuristics.

Once you separate them, the profession's own rhetoric starts to sound strange. "First, do no harm" is a principle about interventions. It has been quietly extended into something closer to *first, gather no information that might lead someone to take an action*, which is not the same principle and does not follow from it. Getting a scan is not an intervention. Looking at your own glucose data is not an intervention. Having continuous vitals monitored is not an intervention. The harm, when it comes, comes from what is done in response to the information — which is precisely why the two deserve separate rules rather than one shared presumption.

Collapsing them does not produce caution. It produces paralysis, and then dresses the paralysis in the language of prudence.

## The real objection, which is not philosophical

I said the easy version of this chapter is wrong, and this is where that matters most.

The actual reason clinicians resist more data is not that they believe less information is better. Almost none of them believe that, and if you ask directly they will say so. The reason is that they have no good way to process what they would receive.

A continuous glucose monitor generates thousands of data points. If a physician has to eyeball raw traces during a fifteen-minute appointment, abundance is not a gift. It is a burden, a medico-legal liability, and one more thing that will not fit in the visit. The instinct to refuse it is not obscurantism. It is triage, and triage is a skill.

But notice what kind of problem that is. If the same system says *A1C equivalent 7.2 percent, nocturnal hypoglycemia clustering on Tuesday nights, likely related to Monday evening dosing* — then abundance becomes useful immediately and obviously, and no clinician on earth would refuse it.

Healthcare has abundance at the collection layer and extreme scarcity at the interpretation layer. We have monitors, wearables, cheap sequencing, ambient capture. We have one clinician's attention for fifteen minutes. The mismatch between those two numbers is the whole problem, and it is an infrastructure problem rather than a fundamental constraint.

This is worth stating plainly, because it changes what the argument is asking for. I am not asking clinicians to absorb more. Asking a physician to review a thousand data points in a fifteen-minute visit is not a reform; it's a joke with a bad punchline. I am saying that the correct response to "we cannot process this" is to build the processing layer — and that the refusal has been standing in for the building for about two decades now.

## The reframe

The calculation medicine currently runs is: *what is the harm of this extra test?* Radiation. False positives. Cascading procedures. Patient anxiety. Cost. Each of those is real, and each is countable, which is part of why they dominate the conversation.

The reframe is: *what is the harm of not having this information when we need it?* The patient whose cancer would have been visible and wasn't looked for. The diabetic whose dangerous pattern was invisible between quarterly checks. The cardiac event that continuous monitoring would have flagged and standard vitals missed.

These harms are real too. They are simply not attributable. Nobody writes a case report about the diagnosis that was never made, because there is nothing to write about — the patient went home, and the thing that was going to happen happened later, to a different doctor, in a different building, with no visible line connecting the two.

So the ledger is kept in a currency that systematically favors one side. The costs of information are itemized. The costs of its absence dissolve into the general background of people getting sick, which is what we expect people to do anyway.

I am not asking for the burden of proof to be reversed. I am asking for it to be symmetrical. Show me evidence that abundant data causes net harm in a given modality, and show me evidence that it produces net benefit, and let each modality prove itself either way. Right now the asymmetry is the default: abundance must demonstrate benefit, while scarcity is assumed safe, and the assumption is never itself put to the test.

CGMs won this argument with evidence rather than rhetoric. Studies showed better outcomes. The skeptics were proven wrong by data about data. Early cancer detection and longitudinal health tracking will have to win the same way, modality by modality, and they should. What they should not have to do is win against a presumption that was never examined.

## What this costs on the receiving end

Everything above is an argument about institutions. Here is the version that matters, which is what the reflex feels like from the chair.

You are told a test isn't necessary. That may be entirely correct. It may also be the output of a reimbursement rule, a training reflex, or a fifteen-minute visit with no room in it — and from where you are sitting, all four are indistinguishable. They arrive in the same sentence, in the same tone, from a person you have no particular reason to doubt.

You ask for your own data and meet friction that is difficult to interpret. The stated reason is that you might misunderstand it, or worry unnecessarily, or make a bad decision. Sometimes that concern is genuine and well founded. But the paternalism is barely concealed even when it is well meant, and patients hear it clearly. The message is that information about your body is yours in principle and someone else's in practice.

This, more than any individual technology fight, is what I think the profession is actually risking. When physicians reflexively dismiss things patients find valuable, and when the gap between what is technically possible and what is clinically permitted grows wide enough to see from outside, patients do not conclude that medicine is being careful. They conclude that medicine is hiding something, or protecting something, or simply isn't interested. And then they leave — not the healthcare system, which they still need, but its authority, which they stop consulting first.

Where that goes is the subject of Chapter Fourteen, so I'll leave it there rather than argue it twice. For now the narrower point is enough: a reflex that was built to protect people is being experienced by those people as indifference, and the profession has almost no way of noticing that, because nothing it measures would show it.

## What restraint was actually for

I said at the start that the parsimony reflex does real work, and I want to end there rather than pretend otherwise.

The scarcity mentality is load-bearing for quality in the current system. It prevents genuine waste. It prevents genuine harm. It is the mechanism that separates the clinician who reasons from the clinician who orders, and nobody has built a replacement for that function. You cannot simply remove it and expect the structure to stand.

So the argument is not: abandon restraint. The argument is that restraint was always about *action*, and somewhere along the way it was extended to cover *information*, and the extension was never examined because it never had to be. When tests were expensive and invasive, the two moved together closely enough that nobody needed to separate them. They do not move together anymore. A sleep mask that logs biometrics passively and cheaply has no meaningful relationship to an exploratory laparotomy, and a rule that governs both is not a rule. It is a habit with a rule's reputation.

Separating them gives medicine a way to become pro-data without becoming reactive and wasteful. Keep the discipline exactly where it belongs, on the decision to act. Move the default on gathering, one modality at a time, as each proves itself — and hold the profession to noticing when it does.

The culture will not change through argument. It will change the way it changed for CGMs and for pulse oximetry: through specific victories that accumulate until the old posture becomes untenable. Virtues do not yield to reasoning. They yield when the world makes the old virtue obsolete and the new one necessary.

Which raises the obvious objection, and it is a good one. If the parsimony instinct is this well founded, and the harms of overtesting this well documented, why should anyone accept an argument for abundance from someone who has not yet watched a patient absorb the consequences of an incidental finding?

That is the right question, and it deserves a full answer rather than a paragraph. The next chapter is that answer.

# The Steelman

A second-year medical student writing a chapter about what his profession gets wrong should expect a specific response, and I would rather meet it here than have it follow the book around.

The response is: *you haven't seen anything yet.* You have not watched a patient go down the cascade. You have not sat with someone through the biopsy that found nothing and the complication that followed it. You have read about incidentalomas; you have not caused one. Come back in ten years and see whether you still think the profession is being precious about data.

That objection is not a dodge. It is the strongest thing anyone can say to me, and part of it is simply true — I said as much before the book started, and I'd rather concede it twice than pretend it stops being true because I've written a chapter since.

But "you'll understand later" is not an argument. It is a prediction about my future mental state, and it has the awkward property of being unfalsifiable in the present. If it turns out I'm wrong, I want to be shown wrong with evidence rather than seniority. So this chapter takes the four strongest objections to the previous one, states each in the best form I can manage, and answers them on the merits. Where the objection wins, I say so.

## The incidentaloma problem is real, and I have underweighted it

**The objection.** The incidentaloma critique isn't theoretical hand-wringing. Aggressive screening of asymptomatic populations can produce net harm through the cascade of interventions on clinically insignificant findings. A shadow on a scan becomes a follow-up scan, which becomes a biopsy, which becomes a bleed, which becomes a hospitalization, in a person who was never going to be troubled by the thing that started it. This isn't doctors being backwards. It is evidence-based concern about iatrogenic harm, and it is the concern that most reliably separates people who have done this work from people who have read about it.

**Where it wins.** Entirely, on the facts. The cascade is real, it is documented, and it hurts people who would otherwise have been fine. Nothing in the previous chapter should be read as denying it. If the choice were between the current regime and a world where every asymptomatic adult gets a whole-body scan and the findings are managed the way findings are managed today, the current regime is safer. I would pick it too.

**Where I still disagree.** The critique assumes that current management protocols for ambiguous findings are fixed. They are not. The cascade is a protocol-design failure, and it is being treated as an inherent property of information.

Watch how the same argument would have sounded in 2001. Physicians could have said, accurately: continuous glucose data will show fluctuations that don't matter, patients will panic, they will over-treat, and some of them will be harmed by the over-treatment. That prediction wasn't crazy. What we did about it, eventually, was develop better interpretation guidelines, better patient education, and better decision thresholds — we solved the protocol problem rather than refusing the data. Nobody now argues the correct response was to withhold glucose readings from diabetics until the interpretation problem was solved in advance.

The incidentaloma cascade deserves the same treatment. If whole-body imaging produces management challenges, the answer is better management protocols. Treating the cascade as a reason not to look is a category error dressed as clinical wisdom.

And the harm calculus remains suspiciously one-sided. Every analysis of incidentaloma risk carefully quantifies the harms of false positives and downstream interventions. The harms of missed diagnoses due to information scarcity are systematically underweighted, because they are invisible — we never learn about the cancer that would have been caught early, the aneurysm that would have been monitored, the condition that was preventable until it wasn't. Absence of evidence gets treated as evidence of absence. "We don't have randomized trials proving whole-body screening improves outcomes" becomes "therefore assume it's harmful" rather than "therefore study it properly." That asymmetry is not a finding. It is a habit.

## The CGM analogy doesn't fully transfer

**The objection.** Continuous glucose monitoring worked because diabetes has clear, actionable thresholds and because frequent monitoring demonstrably improves outcomes. Whole-body screening has no comparable evidence base. The question isn't "is data good?" — it's "does this specific data source improve outcomes net of harms?" That is an empirical question, and it has to be answered separately for every modality. Generalizing from CGMs to imaging is exactly the kind of move a person makes when they have a thesis to defend.

**Where it wins.** On the demand for modality-by-modality evidence, completely. I am not arguing that every monitoring technology deserves adoption because one did. The previous chapter said each modality must prove itself and I meant it. If whole-body screening turns out to produce net harm in properly designed trials, it should not be standard, and I will have been wrong about that modality.

**Where I still disagree.** The objection proves my point more than it refutes it. Yes, CGMs had to demonstrate outcome improvements through studies, and they did. But the establishment resisted *running those studies* because of prior assumptions about too much data. The resistance came before the evidence phase, not in response to it. That is the cultural problem I'm naming.

"Whole-body imaging doesn't have the same evidence base yet" is technically true and quietly misleading. The question is whether the default posture should be *probably harmful until proven otherwise* or *worth studying rigorously*. Medicine currently defaults to the first, and the default itself suppresses the evidence generation that would settle the matter. That is a closed loop, and closed loops are not neutral.

There is also a subtler problem with the claim that diabetes has clear actionable thresholds. It has them now. Some of them we learned *from* continuous monitoring — the technology revealed patterns we didn't know to look for, including nocturnal patterns that quarterly testing structurally could not see. You cannot always know in advance which information will prove actionable, which is the entire epistemic case for exploratory data collection. Requiring a modality to demonstrate actionability before you are allowed to gather the data that would demonstrate it is not rigor.

## The false-positive math is not medical conservatism

**The objection.** Screen a low-prevalence condition in an asymptomatic population and most positives will be false. This is basic epidemiology. A test with ninety-five percent specificity sounds excellent until you apply it to a million healthy people and generate fifty thousand false alarms, each attached to a frightened person and a follow-up pathway. The math here is real, not a cultural artifact, and no amount of enthusiasm about abundance repeals Bayes.

**Where it wins.** The math is the math, and any argument that ignores it disqualifies itself. This is also, not coincidentally, the objection I am least qualified to wave away — the arithmetic doesn't care how confident I am. Anyone making my argument owes the reader an acknowledgment that positive predictive value in low-prevalence populations is genuinely brutal, and that the intuition "more testing finds more disease" is false in exactly the way the objection says.

**Where I still disagree.** Three things.

First, we already accept false-positive burdens whenever we judge the benefit worth it. Mammography in average-risk women generates substantial false positives. We do it anyway, because the lives saved are judged to outweigh the harms. PSA screening generates a large false-positive rate, and the debate has been about whether the tradeoff is favorable, not whether false positives exist. The question is never "do false positives exist." It is "does the benefit-harm ratio favor testing" — and we cannot know that ratio for a new modality without gathering enough data to compute it.

Second, the objection assumes we are stuck with current sensitivity and specificity profiles and current management protocols. Better imaging, better biomarkers, better interpretation, better guidelines for handling ambiguous findings all move the ratio. Treating today's operating characteristics as permanent is precisely the static thinking the previous chapter was about.

Third, the false-positive argument is applied selectively, and the selectivity is revealing. Nobody argues we should stop checking vitals every four hours in hospitals on the grounds that most abnormal readings are false alarms. We accept those false positives because we believe catching the true positives matters. The interesting question is why the same reasoning doesn't extend to other abundant data sources, and I don't think the answer is epidemiological.

## "First, do no harm" is a real principle, not a rhetorical shield

**The objection.** Framing clinical caution as a status game or a training artifact is a cheap move. Physicians hold back because they have internalized an obligation not to hurt people, which is the oldest and best thing about the profession. Recasting that obligation as institutional self-protection is both unfair and corrosive, and it is the kind of argument someone makes when they want permission to do something the obligation forbids.

**Where it wins.** As a warning about tone, and I take it seriously enough to have rewritten parts of the previous chapter because of it. When I wrote that I was embarrassed by physicians dismissing a new scanner, that was a real reaction, and it was also uncharitable in a way I would resent if aimed at me. Most physicians resisting new data sources are not protecting status. They are protecting patients with the tools and time they have, inside a system that gives them fifteen minutes and holds them liable for what they miss. Any version of my argument that requires assuming bad faith is a weaker version.

**Where I still disagree.** "First, do no harm" applies to interventions. That is what it has always meant. Getting an image is not an intervention. Looking at your own glucose data is not an intervention. Having continuous vitals monitored is not an intervention. The harm arrives through subsequent action taken in response to information — which is exactly why the data/action distinction is doing real work rather than playing with words.

The principle has been quietly extended into *first, gather no information that might lead someone to act*, and the extension has never been argued for, only inherited. You can gather abundantly and still exercise complete restraint about what you do next. Those are separable, and separating them is not a loophole. It is the point.

## What would change my mind

Stating this seems only fair, since I have spent two chapters asking the profession to hold its beliefs to a standard.

If well-designed trials of multi-cancer early detection show net harm at population scale — not just false positives, but worse mortality or morbidity in the screened arm — the abundance argument loses its most important case, and I should say so publicly rather than retreating to the position that the protocols were implemented badly.

If patient access to raw data demonstrably produces worse outcomes at scale — more anxiety-driven utilization, worse adherence, worse health — then the paternalism I've been criticizing is empirically justified and I am wrong about one of the things I feel most strongly about.

If interpretation layers turn out not to work — if the summarization problem proves genuinely hard rather than merely unbuilt, so that abundant data remains unusable inside a real clinical encounter for another decade — then the practical case collapses even if the principle survives, and a principle that cannot be practiced is not much of a principle.

I don't expect these. But I have written them down, dated, in a book, which is a more expensive way to be wrong than a post I could quietly edit. That seemed like the right price to pay for spending a chapter telling other people to hold their beliefs loosely.

## Where this leaves the argument

Two of the four objections land hard. The incidentaloma cascade is real and I underweighted it. The false-positive math is real and does not care about my thesis. The other two — that the CGM analogy doesn't transfer, and that clinical caution is being unfairly recast — are partly right about tone and, I think, wrong about substance.

What survives is narrower than what I started with, which is what should happen when you actually argue against yourself rather than performing it.

Gather abundantly. Act selectively. Require each modality to prove itself, and apply the same standard of proof to the claim that scarcity is safe. Build the interpretation layer instead of using its absence as a reason to refuse the data. And stop describing the fifteen-year delay as prudence after the fact, because that is the step that makes the next fifteen-year delay inevitable.

That is a smaller claim than "medicine is anti-abundance and should stop." It is also one I can defend from where I'm standing, which the larger one wasn't.

# What $300,000 in Education Failed to Teach Me

There is a moment in my first year of medical school where I stopped taking notes mid-lecture.

My biostatistics professor had just said something. Not dramatically — almost in passing, the way you mention rain in the forecast. He moved to the next slide. My classmates kept writing.

I sat there with my hand frozen above the keyboard, because I had just realized something that made me quietly furious.

I had paid over two hundred thousand dollars for an undergraduate education at a good school. I took statistics. I got solid grades. I learned p-values, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, the whole apparatus. And nobody, not once in four years, had told me the most important thing about any of it.

## The gap you don't notice until you do

Here is what every college statistics course teaches: how to determine whether a result is statistically significant. You learn the threshold, you learn the tests, you learn to calculate whether something is probably real or probably chance.

Here is what they don't teach: that *real* and *meaningful* are completely different questions.

My professor said it plainly. "Statistical significance tells you if an effect exists. Clinical significance tells you if it matters."

Then he moved on. As though that weren't the entire point.

I raised my hand and asked him to go back to the slide. After class I approached him.

"That distinction — that's the most important thing you said today, isn't it?"

He smiled, a little wearily. "I hope so. I try to emphasize it."

"I don't think it landed. You said it the same way you said everything else."

"You're probably right."

I've thought about that exchange more than almost anything else from that year. He knew. He had known for a long time. He said it every year and watched it fail to land every year, and had apparently made peace with the failure in a way I found genuinely upsetting.

## When true stops meaning anything

Consider what the distinction actually implies. You can prove something works — mathematically, rigorously, published in a peer-reviewed journal — and it can still be useless.

A company studies a weight-loss supplement. Ten thousand participants, six months. Result: the supplement group loses eight-tenths of a pound more than placebo.

With ten thousand participants that is statistically significant. It is real. Not luck, not noise, not measurement error. The supplement genuinely causes slightly more weight loss.

The marketing writes itself. *Clinically proven. Statistically significant results.* Both statements are true.

Also true: eight-tenths of a pound over six months is nothing. You could lose that by skipping dessert twice.

Significance says the effect is real. Significance says nothing about whether the effect matters. My education taught me to detect the first and never taught me to ask the second.

## The mechanism that breaks people's brains

Here is the part that changes how you read everything afterward.

**With enough participants you can prove almost anything has an effect, no matter how small.**

Sample size and detectable effect size are inversely related. Study a hundred people and you can only detect large effects. Study ten thousand and you can detect tiny ones. Study a hundred thousand and you can demonstrate that almost anything does *something*.

The mathematics does not care whether that something matters.

Which means that every headline announcing that a study proves X may be technically accurate and practically meaningless. Not fraud. Not bad science. Just the natural consequence of how significance testing works when you scale the sample.

## What it looks like in a real decision

Your doctor suggests a statin. "Studies show it reduces heart attack risk."

The studies are real. The effect is real. Statistically significant, replicated, solid evidence.

What the studies show is that for people with no prior heart disease, you need to treat roughly a hundred people with statins for five years to prevent one nonfatal heart attack — and around a hundred and fifty to prevent one stroke. Not one death. One heart attack. [^10]

So a hundred people take a daily medication for five years. They deal with potential side effects. They spend money on pills and visits. They become, in a small but real way, patients — people who take something every morning because a professional told them to.

And one of them benefits. The other ninety-nine were going to be fine anyway.

Is that good medicine? I genuinely don't know. It depends entirely on how a given person weighs the tradeoffs. If you're frightened of heart attacks and don't mind pills, maybe it's clearly worth it. If you'd rather not medicalize your life for a one-in-a-hundred shot, maybe not. Both positions are defensible, and the statistics don't resolve the disagreement. They only quantify it well enough that you can disagree precisely.

What makes me want to flip a table is this: my education taught me to trust *statistically significant* as a seal of quality without ever teaching me that significance says nothing about magnitude. And I went into a profession where I would be the one saying it to people.

## The question that should be taught first

Medicine has a metric for exactly this. Number needed to treat: how many people must be treated for one person to benefit.

Some calibration, with the caveat that every one of these numbers depends on the population and the endpoint being counted. Antibiotics for strep throat, single digits. Blood pressure medication after a stroke, low double digits. Aspirin given during an acute heart attack, a few dozen. Statins for primary prevention in low-risk people, around a hundred for a nonfatal heart attack — falling to forty or seventy in higher-risk groups. [^11]

All of these interventions are statistically significant. All of them work in the technical sense. And a number needed to treat in the single digits versus one in the hundreds describes wildly different clinical realities — one is close to a cure, the other is a lottery ticket with side effects.

I don't know whether other fields have an equivalent metric. They should, because it's the only question that finally matters: *how much do you have to do to get one unit of the thing you actually care about?*

## Why nobody teaches this first

I've thought a lot about why the gap exists, and I don't think it's negligence.

Statistics courses teach machinery — how to run tests, calculate p-values, interpret intervals. The mechanics are complex enough to fill a semester. By the time you've learned how to determine whether something is significant, there's no time left to ask what significance means.

Or maybe it's simpler than that. Teaching a procedure is easier than teaching judgment. You can test whether a student computes a p-value correctly. You cannot easily test whether they can weigh whether a finding is worth acting on. So the curriculum optimizes for what it can grade, which is a specific instance of the incentive problem from Chapter Five wearing academic robes.

The result is people who leave university able to detect effects without being able to evaluate whether those effects matter. That is not a gap. It's a chasm, and it runs directly through the middle of every conversation a physician has with a patient about whether to start a medication.

## The pattern generalizes

This isn't unique to statistics.

We teach people to write code without teaching them to ask whether the code should exist. We teach financial modeling without teaching when a model misleads more than it illuminates. We teach argumentation without teaching when to change your mind.

The technical skill is easier to package, test, and grade. The judgment is harder to systematize. And the judgment is the entire point.

## What I'm left with

I'm now roughly six hundred thousand dollars into my education, counting both degrees. The single most valuable thing I've learned might be the distinction between statistical and clinical significance — the idea that numbers can be true without being meaningful.

Not anatomy. Not biochemistry. Not diagnostic algorithms. That.

It should have been week one of introductory statistics. Instead it was an aside in a medical school lecture that most people missed, because nothing in the delivery signaled it was the point.

I caught it because I happened to be paying attention at the right moment. My classmates didn't, and not because they aren't brilliant — they are the fastest learners I have ever been around. They missed it for the same reason I nearly did. It was said in the same tone as everything else.

## What this means for the rest of the book

If training has a gap this large in something this central, the reader should want to know what else it has. That's most of what the remaining chapters are about.

But there's a sharper implication for the argument in Chapter Six. I've spent several chapters asking for more data to reach patients. This chapter is the reason that request carries an obligation: information handed to someone who has been taught to read *significant* as *important* isn't empowerment. It's a new way to be misled, and the misleading will feel authoritative because it comes with numbers attached.

Data abundance without this distinction produces confident, well-documented, badly reasoned decisions at scale. Which is roughly what we have now — except the people making them have medical degrees.

# The Compression Loss Problem

The last few chapters argued for gathering more. This one is about what happens after you have it, and it is the reason the abundance argument needs a second half or it becomes the thing it criticizes.

There is a peculiar madness in how we have been taught to read, and it turns out to be the same madness in how we have been taught to work a patient up. The belief is that understanding comes through dissection — that you approach a text, or a person, like a forensic pathologist with a scalpel, slicing open every sentence, examining every finding under fluorescent light, cataloguing every comma. We call this close reading. We call it thoroughness. We call it rigor.

I think it is a profound misunderstanding of how meaning works, and the mathematics is against it.

## How you actually learned to understand anything

Consider how you learned your first language. You didn't parse grammatical structures. You didn't analyze morphology or diagram sentences. You absorbed meaning through context, repetition, and immersion — something far more holistic than analysis. A child hears *don't touch that, it's hot* and learns not only the literal content but the urgency in the voice, the protective intent, the relationship between touching and pain. The meaning doesn't live in the words. It lives in the whole gestalt of the communication.

Somewhere along the way we decided that this natural capacity was insufficient. We decided true comprehension required breaking things into smaller and smaller pieces, as though meaning were the sum of its parts and understanding were a matter of inventory rather than insight.

This is not how ideas have traveled through human history. The great philosophical traditions were oral for generations before they were written. The Iliad was sung for centuries before anyone put it to parchment. The meaning wasn't in the words; the words were the vehicle for something larger that lived in the telling and the hearing and the space between speaker and listener.

Over-analyzing a text makes the same category error a mechanic would make by taking apart a violin to understand a symphony. Yes, you need the violin, and it must be built a particular way for the music to be possible. But the music isn't in the wood or the strings or the varnish. It emerges from the relationship between instrument, musician, composer, and listener. Disassemble the violin and you have destroyed the thing you claimed to be studying.

## Compression loss

Take a JPEG. Compress it, export it, upload it, download it, compress it again, re-export, upload somewhere else, download, compress once more. Do this enough times and something terrible happens. Artifacts appear. Edges blur. Colors shift. Details vanish. Eventually the image is nearly unrecognizable — and the original meaning was lost not through one catastrophic error but through the accumulated damage of too many operations.

Each individual operation seemed harmless. Several seemed necessary. The cumulative effect is destruction.

That is the shape of the problem, and it has a precise mathematical form.

## Every operation is another test

Watch constitutional scholars argue about the Second Amendment. Hours on comma placement. Does *well regulated* mean government oversight or merely well-functioning? What did *militia* mean in 1791? Does *bear* mean carry, or something narrower about military service? Each word becomes a battleground, each interpretation spawns counter-interpretations, and the debate fractals into infinity.

Here is what that method is doing to itself. With each word dissected, it introduces another operation — another opportunity for error. And not error in the casual sense. Error in the precise statistical sense of a false positive that accumulates when you run too many tests.

Think about multiple hypothesis testing. Run one test at ninety-five percent confidence and you have a five percent chance of a false positive. Run twenty and you are nearly guaranteed to find something significant that is only noise. The mathematics is not in dispute. The more operations you perform, the more likely you are to find patterns that aren't there.

Now apply it to reading. Every word isolated for special scrutiny is a new test, a new opportunity to find meaning nobody intended. Constitutional scholars aren't recovering the true meaning of the Second Amendment through word-by-word analysis. They are generating interpretive artifacts — products of the method rather than properties of the text.

The same thing afflicts religious scholarship, where lifetimes go into parsing individual words and building arguments on arguments until the edifice is a house of cards in which removing any single reading collapses the whole structure.

## The divergence problem

There's a deeper issue underneath. Humans don't share identical definitions for words even within the same culture and language. We operate with fuzzy, overlapping understanding — rough shapes rather than precise definitions.

Ask me to define *understand* and I'll give you an answer. Ask yourself and you'll give a different one. Not wildly different; we share a linguistic space. But the exact definition, pixel by pixel? Different.

This isn't a bug in human communication. It's the feature that makes communication possible. We don't need perfect definitional alignment to transmit meaning, only good-enough alignment. The gestalt is enough.

Force word-by-word analysis and you demand an exact definition for every word. Since mine differs slightly from yours, and yours from the next person's, each word becomes a point of divergence. One word, one divergence. Ten words, ten. A full paragraph compounds it dozens of times, and by the end you are no longer discussing the same text. You are discussing the artifacts your method produced.

And the divergences don't add. They interact and multiply. A five percent margin on one word and five percent on the next doesn't give you ten percent across the phrase. By the time you've parsed a sentence word by word your cumulative divergence from the intended meaning can be most of the meaning.

Define *understand* with *comprehend* and *grasp* and *perceive* — but now those need defining. Define *comprehend* with *understand* and *recognize*. Notice the loop. Each word is defined by words that themselves need defining. It's turtles all the way down, and at every level you introduce new divergence.

The tragedy is that everyone involved believes they are being more rigorous. They think word-by-word analysis brings them closer to truth. The mathematics says every additional operation pushes them further away.

## The same error, wearing a white coat

Now the part that made me want to write this chapter.

A patient arrives. We take a history, examine them, and then we do what training taught us to do: we decompose. Chief complaint. Review of systems, organ by organ. Labs, imaging, a problem list numbered one through eleven. Each finding gets its own line, its own workup, its own plan. The chart grows. The decomposition is thorough, and thoroughness is the thing we are graded on.

Somewhere in that process the person stops being a person and becomes a list.

I want to be careful here, because decomposition is not optional in medicine and I am not arguing for intuition over evidence. You cannot treat a potassium of 6.8 holistically. The problem list exists because it works, and a clinician who refuses to itemize will miss things that itemizing catches.

But watch what the multiple-comparisons math does inside a workup, because it is not a metaphor here. It is literally the same mathematics.

Order a comprehensive metabolic panel, a complete blood count, a thyroid panel, vitamin levels, and tumor markers on a person with a headache, and you have run dozens of tests, each with its own reference range built to include ninety-five percent of a healthy population. Run enough of them on a healthy person and you are *guaranteed* to produce an abnormal value. Not because anything is wrong. Because that is what a ninety-fifth percentile cutoff does when you sample it repeatedly.

The incidentaloma is a Type I error with a radiology report attached.

This is what the parsimony reflex from Chapter Six was actually protecting, and it is the strongest form of that argument — stronger, I think, than the version its own defenders usually offer. The impressive attending who needs fewer data points isn't just demonstrating competence. They are running fewer tests against a noisy background, and therefore generating fewer artifacts. The scarcity instinct is, among other things, an intuitive correction for multiple comparisons that medicine arrived at long before it had the statistical language to describe it.

Which means Chapter Six was incomplete as written. If you simply gather more without changing how you read, the math guarantees you drown in artifacts, and every objection the skeptics raised comes true.

## What the fix actually is

The resolution isn't to gather less. It's to stop treating the workup as an inventory and start treating it as a synthesis — which is precisely what the best clinicians already do, and what almost nothing in the system supports them in doing.

Watch a good attending take a set of findings apart and then put them back together. They don't work down the problem list item by item. They hold the whole picture and ask what single story would produce this constellation. The mildly abnormal sodium, the fatigue, the medication list, the fact that the patient has been drinking more water since a friend told her to — these stop being four line items and become one explanation. The synthesis discards most of the individual findings, not because they are wrong, but because they were never independently meaningful.

That move is the clinical equivalent of reading a sentence rather than its words. And notice that it *requires* abundance to work well. You cannot synthesize a picture from three data points; you get a better gestalt from more information, provided you are reading it as a picture rather than a list.

So the two halves fit together, and only together:

**Gather abundantly.** Information is cheap and a fuller picture supports better synthesis.

**Act selectively.** Intervention carries real risk, and most findings should change nothing.

**Read holistically.** Interpret the whole rather than pursuing each finding as an independent hypothesis, because independent pursuit is what manufactures artifacts.

Chapter Six argued the first two and treated the third as an infrastructure problem — build the interpretation layer. That's still true, and it's why summarization matters more than collection. But it isn't only an engineering problem. It's an epistemological one, and it has a name in medicine already. We call it clinical judgment, and we treat it as something mystical that accrues with years rather than something specific that could be taught.

It is not mystical. It is the refusal to run twenty tests on a text.

## What this looks like from the chair

You have felt this if you have ever been thoroughly worked up and still not understood.

Every system examined. Every value returned. A folder of results and a follow-up scheduled for the incidental thing on your liver that is almost certainly nothing but requires monitoring, and now belongs to you permanently. Nobody was careless. Everyone was thorough. And you leave with more findings than you arrived with and less sense of what is happening to you.

That is compression loss, experienced from inside. The information about you was decomposed, and no one performed the reassembly, because the reassembly is unbilled, unmeasured, and takes the one resource the visit does not have.

The patient who says *nobody is looking at the whole picture* is not being difficult. They are making a technically precise complaint, and they are usually right.

## The limit of the method

Two centuries of medicine have been a story of learning to see smaller things — cells, then molecules, then variants. That reductionist program has been the most successful research strategy in the history of the field and I am not arguing against it. Nothing here says stop decomposing.

It says that decomposition is a method rather than an understanding, and that a method optimized for discovery is not automatically the right method for care. The lab that finds the mechanism and the clinician who treats the person are running different operations, and medicine has been quietly assuming they're the same operation performed at different scales.

They are not. One takes things apart to learn what they are made of. The other has to put a person back together well enough to say something true about them in fifteen minutes.

We train almost exclusively for the first and then grade people on the second.

And we start earlier than I've let on in this chapter. Everything above describes what happens to a patient in front of a clinician. But the same decomposition happens to the knowledge itself, years before anyone sees a patient — thousands of facts drilled as discrete units, with the connective structure between them left to assemble itself or not. Most of my classmates could reproduce any individual fragment on command. Fewer could say what held the fragments together, and nothing in the training was designed to ask them.

I have more to say about that, and it belongs at the end of this book rather than here, because it's the one part of this argument I stopped writing about and started building.

# What a $1 MRI Would Actually Tell You

A friend and I had one of those arguments that starts casual and turns into a real mental workout. He posed what he thought was a simple question.

*Imagine a future where an MRI costs one dollar, and it got there through an ordinary competitive market — no subsidy, no price control, no distortion. Is that obviously a win?*

At first glance the answer seems trivial. Lower cost, more access, better outcomes. Every argument in this book so far points that direction: cheap imaging is exactly the bottom rung of the escalation ladder I've been arguing medicine is missing.

I sat with it while he kept talking, and I came to think the obvious answer is wrong — and wrong in a way that matters for how anyone should read price and access signals in healthcare. This chapter is a reasoning tool rather than an argument about medicine. It's the last one before the case studies, and the case studies need it.

## What a price actually is

To see the problem you have to be clear about what a price represents.

In a functioning market, the price of a good reflects more than the cost of producing it. It emerges from two forces: what it costs to supply, and how much people value it relative to everything else they could buy instead.

Price is not a measure of cost. It's a measure of scarcity and relative value at the margin — how much this good is prioritized against all available alternatives.

That's why luxury goods stay expensive even when they're cheap to produce: demand holds. And it's why older technologies see their prices collapse when something better arrives, even while they still work exactly as well as they did before.

## Could production savings alone explain it?

The obvious explanation for a one-dollar MRI is that MRIs got radically cheaper to make.

Follow that through. If production costs collapsed but the MRI remained the most valuable diagnostic tool available, what would manufacturers do? Keep prices high and enjoy the margin. In any market where a product stays relatively scarce and highly valued, sellers have no reason to collapse their prices, because buyers are still willing to pay.

Prices don't fall to near-zero because production got cheap. They fall because something forced them down.

## What would actually force it

In a competitive market there are only a few paths to a one-dollar MRI, and they travel together. New diagnostic technologies emerge that do the job better. Clinicians and patients shift toward them. MRI manufacturers face competition and cut prices to stay relevant. The MRI is no longer scarce or highly prioritized, so its price drops toward marginal cost.

In other words, a one-dollar MRI tells you that *other tools have surpassed it*. If MRI were still the best available answer in most situations, prices wouldn't collapse to a dollar, because plenty of buyers would still pay a hundred or five hundred for the thing they needed most.

The price isn't describing the machine. It's describing the machine's position relative to everything else.

## Why cheaper isn't automatically better for the patient

You can accept all of that and still object: even if MRIs are relatively less valued now, isn't cheap access still good? More people get scans. How is that bad?

Here's the trap. A price collapse of that magnitude implies competition, and in this case it implies that newer diagnostics are meaningfully superior. Which means that in the world where MRIs cost a dollar, three things are simultaneously true. Other tools are delivering better outcomes. The MRI is used in fewer cases, or in more marginal ones. And most patients are being served by those better alternatives rather than by the cheap MRI.

So the one-dollar MRI isn't a more useful MRI. It's a signal that better options exist — and the patient who is excited about cheap MRIs may be reaching for the tool everyone else has already moved past.

The size of the collapse carries information too. A trivial improvement wouldn't push MRI to a dollar; manufacturers could still charge a hundred and find buyers. A collapse that severe implies the alternatives aren't marginally better. They're dramatically better.

## The general skill

This is the most common failure in reasoning about any market, and it isn't really about MRIs. It's about failing to update.

When you observe a one-dollar MRI you cannot keep your prior belief that the MRI is central to care. The price collapse is *evidence* that the market shifted. The more extreme the drop, the stronger the evidence that the thing has been displaced.

You observe new data: MRIs now cost a dollar in a competitive market. You update: we are probably in a world where superior technologies exist and MRI plays a diminished role.

Think of rapid antigen tests against PCR. Antigen tests are cheap and fast and not the accuracy standard. If antigen tests were forced to a dollar, that would tell you superior options now dominate serious use cases and antigen testing had been pushed to the periphery. Same logic.

## What this is for

I've put this chapter here because everything in Part Three depends on the reader being able to do this move, and because it protects the book's own argument from a version of itself I don't believe.

The abundance argument is not "cheap is good." Cheap is a signal, and signals have to be read. A technology becoming cheap can mean it has been commoditized and remains valuable — which is the CGM story, where cost fell while clinical importance rose. Or it can mean it has been superseded, which is the one-dollar MRI. Those look identical on a price tag and are opposite situations, and a profession that can't tell them apart will make bad decisions in both directions: dismissing the first as cheapened, and chasing the second as accessible.

The transferable question — the one I now ask about almost everything — is this: *what conditions must be true to produce this outcome?*

Not what caused this. What must be true about the world for this to be the result.

It's the move that turns an observation into evidence. Every case study in the next three chapters runs it: what must be true about medicine for a forty-year adoption lag to be the outcome? What must be true about psychiatry for its moat to hold when radiology's is eroding? What must be true about a company for it to choose a general wellness label over a clearance pathway?

Ask that consistently and the answers stop being opinions.

# On the Midjourney Whole-Body Ultrasound Scanner

Midjourney, the text-to-image company, announced a health division and showed a prototype whole-body ultrasound scanner. Some of the marketing called it "Ultrasonic CT," which is nonsense — no CT, no radiation, nothing to do with either. It runs on licensed ultrasound-on-chip hardware through a deal reportedly worth tens of millions. The pitch: a full internal scan in under a minute, for a few dollars, no radiation, no magnetic field. No FDA clearance for diagnosis. They're shipping it as a general wellness product — body-composition maps, not diagnoses — while pursuing clearance later.

Radiologists were dunking on it within hours. My first reaction wasn't to the scanner. It was to my own people.

I'm embarrassed by the doctors in this debate, and not in a polite professional way. More innovation is better. It will create demand for the gentler intermediate steps we don't have yet. So how is it that so many physicians can't picture a world with *something* between a calcium score and an angiogram? Something between a scan that finds a growth and a PET scan that tells you whether the growth intends to kill you? That isn't futurism. That's how medicine works, and how it has always worked. Cheap and harmless first, sharp and invasive only for what the cheap stuff flags. That's the whole game. We invented it.

Then I spent a few weeks actually reading, and the chapter I thought I was going to write turned out to be wrong in a specific and instructive way. Both about the scanner and about the argument I was making.

## The reflex, and what it sounds like from outside

I believe the physicians raising the alarm mean well. I don't think they have any idea how anti-health they sound.

People are already exhausted by us. Every visit is an hour in a waiting room because the system is jammed. Patients gave up expecting better years ago while we wave at "admin" as though the dysfunction were weather rather than something we built and could unbuild. So people stop coming. They stop coming until somebody's mother gets the stage four diagnosis that a scan might have caught at stage one, if scanning at scale had ever been cheap and easy enough to fit inside a normal human life.

It takes almost no imagination to see how *let's restrict access because some patients might get anxious about an ambiguous result* arrives at that outcome. A profession protecting itself from inconvenience and calling it caution.

I used to make this point with an analogy: a teacher who won't let anyone in the room read above a third-grade level because one child can only read at third-grade level. Lock down everyone's access to information because a few people might react in a way that's inconvenient for the institution.

The analogy doesn't hold, and I'd rather say so than let it slide. In a classroom, letting everyone read at their own level costs nothing; a book on a shelf hurts no one. A false positive on a body scan is not free. It's a biopsy, or a surgery, each with a real complication rate, landing on a real person who never had the disease you were hunting. The honest objection from the medical side isn't that patients get anxious. It's that acting on a positive finding carries physical risk, and there is no version of that in a reading-level rule.

I'm keeping the analogy anyway, with its failure attached, because the policy logic — restrict everyone to manage a few — is still the part I'm attacking. But an argument that needs a broken analogy to land is an argument worth checking, so let me check it against the best real-world case *against* my position.

## South Korea, which is the case against me

Starting in the 1990s, South Korea made cheap thyroid ultrasound widely available alongside government-funded cancer screening. By 2011 the rate of thyroid cancer diagnoses was fifteen times the 1993 rate. Mortality did not move. Cancers under two centimeters accounted for 94.4 percent of the increase. [^12]

The scans were finding small, indolent disease that autopsy studies show sits quietly in a large fraction of the population and never does anything. For years the only response on offer was surgery — tens of thousands of thyroidectomies, with their real complication rates, performed on people who were never in danger.

That is not "the data was bad." That is data with no normalization layer doing real harm, which is precisely what my critics say will happen and precisely what did happen.

But sit with the whole case rather than half of it, because both halves matter. The fix was not to stop scanning. The fix was to build the thresholds this chapter argues demand should produce: risk stratification systems that score a nodule before anyone reaches for a needle, plus active surveillance for small low-risk lesions instead of reflexive surgery. That took decades and a great many unnecessary operations to get right.

And it still isn't clean. After public concern mounted, screening and thyroidectomy rates fell sharply — the follow-up in the same journal was titled "Turning the Tide." Whether the pullback carried its own cost in missed aggressive disease is contested and the data is observational. [^13]

So the honest read isn't that screening won or that screening lost. It's that building the right threshold is real, ongoing, expensive work rather than something that materializes the moment the data shows up. I had been treating threshold-building as a footnote to the abundance argument. Korea says it's the main event.

## Caution has a body count too

The reason I still land where I land is that the ledger has a second column, and medicine is much worse at reading it.

In 2012 the US Preventive Services Task Force recommended against routine PSA screening for prostate cancer at any age, largely over overdiagnosis and overtreatment — the same logic now aimed at whole-body scanning. Screening dropped sharply. In the years following, multiple analyses found higher-stage disease and rising metastatic prostate cancer at diagnosis. Mortality, which had been falling for two decades, flattened. The Task Force revised the recommendation in 2018 to shared decision-making for men aged 55 to 69. [^14]

The evidence there is mostly observational rather than a randomized smoking gun, and I won't oversell it. But the direction and the timing line up closely enough to take seriously the proposition that excess caution kills people too, and that its victims are just as real as the woman who had an unnecessary thyroidectomy. They are only harder to count. Korea produced a number; the PSA reversal produced a trend line and an argument about what caused it.

This is the asymmetry the whole book keeps running into. One column is itemized. The other dissolves into the background rate of people getting sick.

## The objection I can't take seriously, and the one I can

The objection I find close to impossible to accept is that we need rigorous study before embracing any of this. You cannot have the definitive study yet, because the thing you'd study doesn't exist in mature form. And the moment it does start to exist — the moment a technology is good enough to generate demand for the next refinement — this exact crowd arrives to block it, strangling the process that would have produced the evidence they say they're waiting for. It's a closed loop.

Except that I overstated it, and the correction matters.

You *can* study an immature screening technology, if you point it at a population whose risk justifies it and track outcomes prospectively. Inova Health System is doing exactly that. Their pilot enrolls eight hundred career and volunteer firefighters aged thirty-five and over with at least ten cumulative years on the job, randomizes them between whole-body MRI and whole-body ultrasound, and screens everyone with a multi-cancer detection assay on top. The design builds in the follow-up question that matters most — what the downstream workup actually looks like when these modalities flag something. [^15]

The population choice is the whole lesson. In July 2022 an International Agency for Research on Cancer working group concluded that occupational firefighting is carcinogenic to humans. [^16] That is what an enriched-risk cohort looks like: not the general public, not the worried wealthy, but a group with a documented occupational exposure and a real prior probability of disease.

Screen those people and the false-positive mathematics that makes population screening so punishing becomes considerably more forgiving, because prevalence is doing work for you rather than against you. This is the answer to my own objection two paragraphs ago, and it did not require anyone to wait for permission or to skip validation. It required picking the right eight hundred people.

That's the model. Deploy where prevalence earns it, watch what happens, let evidence accumulate. The real failure mode isn't "we can't study this yet." It's blasting the technology at a general, unstratified population *before* that study exists. Which is a choice, not fate.

Which brings me, uncomfortably, back to Midjourney.

## The scanner itself deserves the scrutiny

Being generous to a category is not the same as being generous to a company, and I conflated the two for about a week.

Look at what Midjourney is actually doing. No FDA clearance for diagnostic use, and a deliberate general wellness label — a category that conveniently sidesteps the clearance pathway that would have required generating exactly the prospective evidence I keep demanding — all while scaling toward a stated goal of tens of thousands of units worldwide.

That is not a scrappy innovator throttled by stuffy doctors. That is a company skipping a validation route that already exists so it can reach consumers first. It is close enough to a pattern we have seen before to deserve hard scrutiny on its own terms, entirely separate from whether whole-body screening as a category deserves a chance.

So the defensible version of "let's see where this leads" is: let's see where it leads once it's tested the way Inova is testing it. Not once it's been sold to fifty thousand people with no follow-up.

I want to be precise about what I'm doing here, because it would be easy to read this as retreat. The category argument survives intact. Cheap, low-harm, early-stage imaging is the natural bottom rung of an escalation ladder medicine already believes in, and the intermediate rungs between "we found something" and "here is what it is" are genuinely missing. What doesn't survive is my instinct to defend any particular company because its critics annoyed me. The radiologists dunking on Midjourney were, on the specific question of Midjourney's regulatory posture, largely right. They were right for reasons that don't generalize to the category, and I conflated those two things because the tone of the dunking made me want to.

That's worth noticing about myself. A profession's condescension is not evidence that the profession is wrong.

## Why more data is still the right default

Not because data is holy. It isn't; some of the ugliest surveillance states and weapons programs in history were exquisitely data-driven. But every guideline and every evidence-based anything this field has produced came from more data, never less.

"What do we do with this new information" is a completely normal question. It has never once been a good reason not to go get the information. It's the reason *to* go get it, so we can work out what it means and when to act. That is the literal job description of evidence-based medicine. Saying we don't know what to do with this yet so let's not collect it doesn't protect the discipline. It turns the discipline inside out.

And consider the mechanism. With no new data and no demand for the softer steps between an incidentaloma and a diagnosis, where exactly does the technology that fixes this come from? Who funds it? Nobody is arguing for calcium-score-to-angiogram with nothing in between for the fun of it. The point is the opposite: the more these tools spread, the better we get at managing the climb from *huh, what's that* to *here's what it is*. More findings generate more data. More data is demand, and demand is the only thing that has ever conjured better tools into existence.

In the meantime the flood of findings gets handled the hard, unglamorous way — actual counseling, actual coordination, physicians talking to each other to prevent needless escalation. That work is not optional and it is not beneath us. Korea is the proof that skipping it is expensive. Korea is also the proof that it can be built.

## The steelman

There is one genuinely good argument on the medical side and I won't pretend otherwise. Medicine can't swallow every shiny new thing, because being wrong is expensive in human lives, most new tools don't pan out, and you do need solid evidence before telling people something improves outcomes.

Entirely fair. The Midjourney scanner might be a dead end. It might be the first link in a chain of demand ending somewhere genuinely valuable. Nobody knows, including me. That is the point — and it argues for structured trials in enriched populations rather than either dismissal or consumer rollout.

My grandmother died because her cancer was found far too late. So did a lot of people's grandmothers. You can dislike hearing this: when you have buried someone to a late catch and you watch a physician stand in the path of a tool that might have caught it sooner, you do not feel grateful for their prudence. You feel like the person you trusted with your life is the same person blocking the thing that might have saved it.

I know that feeling is not an argument. I have spent this chapter checking it against the strongest evidence I could find, and it survived contact in a modified form: not *let everything through*, but *stop treating the absence of evidence as though it were evidence, and go build the thresholds instead.*

The next few years will be ugly while medicine works through this. I'm walking in anyway. If the cost of catching deadly disease earlier is a stretch of real institutional discomfort while we build the right thresholds and guardrails, that's a price worth paying — and refusing to pay it is the less defensible choice, not the responsible one.

# When Does Technology Kill a Profession

A friend sent our group chat a news article. The CEO of America's largest public hospital system had announced he was ready to replace radiologists with AI.

My response was one line: this will increase demand and pay for radiologists.

What followed was a two-hour argument that touched on ATMs, the iPhone, cardiac surgery, and the entire history of medicine. At some point I realized I had written more in that chat than in most essays I've published, which is usually a sign that the disagreement is about something structural rather than something factual.

The debate about AI and professional obsolescence is nearly always framed as binary. Either the technology kills the job or it doesn't. Either you're a techno-optimist who thinks everything will be fine or a doomer who thinks we're all replaceable. Both camps argue from vibes. Neither has a usable framework for why some professions survive technology and others don't.

I think there are three conditions that determine the answer, plus a fourth thing almost nobody discusses. Together they clarify not just radiology's future but any profession currently watching AI with hope or dread — and they turn out to be a better instrument than the intuition most people are using.

## The paradox that makes the intuitive answer wrong

Before the conditions, Jevons.

In 1865, William Stanley Jevons noticed something that should have been impossible. As steam engines became more fuel-efficient, Britain consumed *more* coal, not less. Efficiency didn't reduce demand. It expanded it, because lower cost unlocked uses nobody had bothered to imagine while coal was expensive. [^17]

The ATM is the modern textbook case. When automated teller machines spread through the 1980s and 1990s, the obvious prediction was fewer tellers. The machine handled deposits, withdrawals, balance checks — the core of the job. And per branch, the prediction held: the average urban branch needed about twenty-one tellers in 1988 and about thirteen by 2004.

But total teller employment *rose* over that period. James Bessen's work on this puts it at roughly 500,000 in the early 1980s to nearly 600,000 by 2010, across a stretch when some 400,000 ATMs were installed nationwide. Cheaper branches meant more branches — urban branch counts rose about 43 percent — and more branches meant more tellers even at lower headcount per location. Jevons won. [^18]

Which produces the comfortable conclusion that technology doesn't kill jobs, it creates them.

Then the iPhone arrived.

Mobile banking didn't automate a task inside branch banking. It eliminated the reason to visit a branch. By 2022, teller employment had fallen from 332,000 full-time positions in 2010 to about 164,000. That wasn't a delayed ATM shock. It was a different technology attacking a different thing — not the task, but the institution the task lived inside.

So the ATM story isn't evidence that technology always creates jobs. It's evidence that the outcome depends on which technology, attacking what, in what context. Which is what the three conditions are for.

## Condition one: is the automation partial or complete?

The most useful question about any technology is not what it can do. It's what it can't do, and whether the remaining human role still matters.

ATMs automated one task within a multi-task job. Tellers still handled loan inquiries, resolved disputes, cross-sold products, maintained relationships. The machine took the repetitive core and the human kept the contextual periphery — and that periphery turned out to be *more* valuable once routine transactions moved to machines. Wages rose. Banks began hiring college graduates for a role that hadn't required a degree.

Mobile banking was different in kind rather than degree. It didn't make branches more efficient; it made the question "how do we staff this branch?" collapse into "why does this branch exist?" When that question flips, there is no peripheral role to retreat into. The institution dissolves and the job goes with it.

For radiology, this is the first diagnostic question. AI is advancing fast on image interpretation, which is the core diagnostic read. But interpretation is a smaller share of the job than outsiders assume. The Vancouver Workload Utilization Evaluation Study put direct image interpretation at 36.4 percent of radiologists' time, with 43.8 percent going to non-interpretive work — protocolling requisitions, supervising studies, image-guided procedures, consulting with other physicians, direct patient care. The same study found radiologists averaging six interactions per hour with other clinical staff, of which more than eighty percent influenced patient care in real time. [^19]

That last detail is the one worth holding onto. A job with six real-time clinical interactions an hour is not primarily a reading job that happens to involve colleagues. It is a consultative job in which reading is the core technical act.

The radiologist who only reads scans is the teller whose only job was counting cash: exposed to the first wave. The radiologist who reads scans and runs tumor boards and performs interventional procedures and advises on imaging strategy is closer to the teller who became a relationship banker, where the machine clarified their value rather than erasing it.

## Condition two: does lower cost expand the market?

Jevons requires an elastic market. Lower cost has to unlock new demand rather than just make existing demand cheaper to serve. This is where the two banking stories diverge most sharply.

ATMs made branches cheaper to run and banks responded by opening more of them — urban branch counts rose roughly forty-three percent over the period. More branches, more customers, more complex transactions, more need for judgment at the window. The efficiency gain grew the pie and kept humans inside it.

Mobile banking also made transactions dramatically cheaper. But it didn't generate more banking activity requiring human presence; it routed existing activity away from the places humans worked. The gain went to consumers and to margins, and it shrank the institutional context those jobs depended on.

For radiology the structural case is stronger than it looks. Global imaging volume is growing faster than radiologist supply. The UK projects a forty percent shortfall in consultant radiologists by 2028; the US faces a projected gap of well over a hundred thousand physicians across specialties by the early 2030s. [^20] Cheaper, faster reads could make imaging more accessible, enable broader screening, and generate volume the current workforce cannot absorb. That's the ATM outcome: efficiency enabling expansion, expansion requiring expertise.

But there's a deeper version of this argument that I think gets missed.

Clayton Christensen makes an observation about medical history that cuts to the center of the debate. Every time medicine developed a new way of seeing something previously invisible, it didn't merely improve care. It discovered new disease. New disease created new clinical categories, and new categories required new specialists. The EKG didn't just help cardiologists see hearts more clearly; it revealed arrhythmias nobody had characterized, which is part of what made cardiology a distinct specialty. The endoscope helped create gastroenterologists. MRI didn't augment neurology so much as birth neuroradiology. [^21]

The pattern is consistent enough to feel like a law. New visibility creates new disease categories, which create new high-value subspecialties.

AI reading imaging at population scale is the next iteration. When a model processes a million chest X-rays it doesn't only read them faster than a human — it finds patterns across thousands of cases that no individual career is long enough to accumulate. Some of those will be noise. Some will describe real pathology that currently has no name, no code, no protocol, and no specialist. Those discoveries will need humans to validate, characterize, study, and eventually treat them, and that work creates subspecialties that are high-acuity and high-value almost by construction.

So the bifurcation argument is more optimistic than it first sounds. The middle of radiology — routine reads on standard cases — faces real pressure. The frontier expands every time AI finds something new. The question isn't whether there will be enough work. It's whether the profession moves fast enough to claim it.

## Condition three: is there a regulatory moat?

The third condition is the one most people forget, and it is the one medicine has in abundance.

Branch banking survived ATMs partly because it was regulated, liability-laden, and structured around physical presence. Customers needed branches for mortgages, disputes, complex products. Some functions had to happen in person by rule. That structure bought tellers decades.

Mobile banking eventually breached the moat — but not by defeating the regulations. It changed what customers wanted so completely that the structure protecting branches became irrelevant. The moat didn't get filled in. The river feeding it changed course.

For radiology the moat is deeper. The most important word in healthcare is licensure. You cannot practice medicine without it. You cannot bill for a diagnostic read without a credentialed physician signing off. You cannot deploy autonomous AI for clinical diagnosis without regulatory clearance, and no FDA-cleared radiology tool is currently approved for fully autonomous diagnosis without physician oversight. [^22]

This is structural rather than incidental. The regulatory architecture around physician practice was built over a century, is defended by organized professional associations, and serves genuine patient-safety interests that make it politically durable. Any scenario where AI fully replaces radiologists requires not just technical capability but regulatory permission, liability restructuring, and coverage changes — each of which moves slowly and faces organized resistance.

The mobile banking lesson is still worth sitting with. If patients and health systems eventually prefer and trust autonomous reads, regulatory structures will follow rather than lead. The moat is real. It is not permanent. But it is almost certainly durable enough to outlast the careers of everyone currently in radiology training, and in a profession with thirty-year careers that is worth something.

## The part nobody is discussing: humans are the training data

Here is the argument that reframes everything, and I haven't seen it made cleanly anywhere.

Think about CAPTCHAs. For roughly twenty years, every time you squinted at a distorted street number or clicked the traffic lights in a grid, you weren't only proving you were human. You were labeling training data. Those billions of interactions, contributed unknowingly by ordinary people, helped train the computer vision models now underneath autonomous vehicles and a significant fraction of modern machine learning. The AI didn't arrive from nowhere. Humans built it, one labeled image at a time, without realizing that's what they were doing.

Radiology is in the same relationship with medical AI, and it changes how the profession's future should be read.

Every scan a radiologist reads and annotates, every report generated, every finding described with clinical precision, becomes potential training data for the next generation of imaging models. The systems that will eventually surpass human performance on standard reads are being trained right now on the output of human radiologists. The AI that threatens the routine work is, quite literally, built from the profession's work product.

That creates a dependency easy to miss. These models don't self-improve in a vacuum. They need new labeled data, especially for rare findings, novel pathology, and edge cases underrepresented in existing sets. The model that surfaces a new cancer phenotype in a population-scale dataset still needs expert radiologists to validate the finding, characterize its significance, and generate the annotated cases that teach the next model to detect it reliably.

And the better the AI gets at finding new things, the more it needs experts to confirm what it found — because validating a potentially novel finding carries much higher stakes than confirming a routine pneumonia. The frontier keeps moving, and the human role at the frontier doesn't shrink. It becomes more consequential with each iteration.

This is why the Christensen loop is self-reinforcing rather than self-terminating. AI finds a pattern. Radiologists validate it. Validation generates labeled data. Labeled data trains better AI. Better AI finds subtler patterns, which require more expert validation. The loop accelerates and the human node becomes more specialized rather than disappearing.

You cannot shortcut this. You cannot train a model to detect a rare cardiac imaging biomarker without enough expert radiologists reading enough cardiac imaging to label enough positive cases. At the frontier, the relationship is genuinely symbiotic rather than substitutional.

The routine overnight reads are the cash-handling teller: under pressure, probably headed for compression. The frontier radiologist sitting at the edge of what AI can currently see is something else — a role that gets *more* valuable as the AI improves.

## The history of medicine is this argument

None of this is novel theory. It's what medicine has been doing for two centuries.

The general practitioner nearly disappeared. In the early 1930s the large majority of American physicians identified as general practitioners; today family and internal medicine physicians — their successors — make up roughly a quarter of the workforce. [^23] Technology didn't kill generalism. It fragmented generalism into dozens of specialties and subspecialties, because each new technology made something visible that hadn't been and generated enough clinical demand to justify a new kind of expert.

Cardiac surgery didn't exist before the bypass machine. On 26 March 1954, C. Walton Lillehei performed the first successful open-heart operation using cross-circulation at the University of Minnesota — connecting the patient's circulation to a living human donor who served as the oxygenator, because mechanical bypass wasn't ready. In the operation that followed a few weeks later, the donor was the patient's own father, and that child lived to sixty-seven. [^24] The bypass machine didn't threaten cardiac surgery. It *was* cardiac surgery.

In none of these cases did technology produce clean elimination. It produced bifurcation, transformation, or creation — outcomes that looked very different depending on where in the profession you sat and how quickly you moved.

## The objection worth taking seriously

A friend pushed back on the whole framework: *you're following the siren song of the people selling this.*

He isn't wrong to push. AI is categorically different from previous technologies because it attacks all three conditions at once and has no obvious ceiling. ATMs had a clear boundary — they couldn't do relationship banking. Mobile banking had one too; it couldn't handle a mortgage dispute requiring judgment. Every prior automation had a functional edge that defined the surviving human role.

With multimodal AI that edge is genuinely unclear. The technology isn't specialized. It synthesizes clinical history, drafts reports, suggests differentials, explains findings to patients — and each capability expansion narrows the periphery a radiologist could retreat into.

My response isn't that he's wrong about the ceiling. It's that the ceiling matters less than the speed at which the surrounding institutions can move. Healthcare cannot move fast and break things. Hospitals don't. Regulators don't. Insurers don't. Boards don't. Branch banking's moat did fall, but it fell over roughly fifteen years, and fifteen years is a long time in a profession with thirty-year careers. The tellers who adapted in 2010 had full careers ahead of them before contraction arrived.

The honest version is this: if AI eventually achieves general-purpose clinical cognition, the three conditions probably cannot hold indefinitely. But the training-data loop provides a floor even then, because general clinical AI still needs humans to validate novel findings. That role is smaller than the current workforce. It is also higher-acuity, better paid, and more interesting — which is, historically, exactly how medicine has responded to every tool it has built.

## What this actually means

The most common mistake in these debates is treating *will technology change this profession* as the same question as *will technology eliminate it*.

Bank tellers changed beyond recognition, from cash handlers to relationship bankers, and then contracted sharply when mobile banking made the branch optional. They weren't eliminated by ATMs. They were transformed by ATMs and then hollowed out by something nobody was watching for when ATMs were deployed.

Cardiac surgeons didn't exist before 1954. They weren't threatened by the bypass machine. They were created by it.

Radiology will follow the same logic. The bottom of the distribution faces real pressure. The top — subspecialists at the frontier of AI-discovered pathology, interventionalists, imaging informaticists — has an entirely different calculus. And underneath both sits the layer most people miss: radiologists aren't merely surviving alongside the AI. They're building it, one annotated finding at a time, the same way a generation of internet users built computer vision by clicking on fire hydrants.

The profession will bifurcate. The middle will hollow out, the frontier will expand, and the radiologists who understand that their work product is the substrate the next model trains on will position themselves accordingly.

Which is, as I said in that group chat, the entire history of medicine.

# Psychiatry and the Double Moat

On my way to a psychiatry rotation I recorded a voice note to myself. It said: psychiatry won't get replaced by AI.

I turned it over for the rest of the drive, and the more I sat with it the more I thought there was something larger in it than one specialty's employment prospects. This chapter is the result. It is deliberately the counter-case — the place where the framework from the last chapter produces an answer opposite to the one it produced for radiology, and where I think medicine's caution is not a reflex but a correct reading of the situation.

The previous chapter asked three questions: is the automation partial or complete, does lower cost expand the market, and is there a regulatory moat. Run psychiatry through them and the answers come back different in a way that matters.

## The core observation

When language models take over cognitive work that used to require a salary, what survives isn't necessarily the most technically sophisticated work. It's the most human work. And some of the most human work happens to sit behind regulatory barriers that make it close to untouchable.

Psychiatry is the clearest example I can find.

What I mean by a double moat is two things working together, and neither alone would be enough. The first is regulatory: prescription rights, controlled-substance authority, licensing boards, malpractice liability. You cannot decide to prescribe lithium because you watched a video and feel confident about it. The second is harder to articulate and probably more durable: an irreducibly human element. Transference. Genuine presence. The fact that when someone is in psychiatric crisis they need a person — not a simulation of one — with actual skin in the game.

Either moat alone is crossable. Regulatory barriers fall when the thing they protect stops mattering to people, which is exactly what happened to branch banking. Purely human work without regulatory protection gets competed down by anything cheap enough and good enough. It's the combination that holds, and psychiatry has both.

## Running the three conditions

**Is the automation partial or complete?** In radiology the automatable core is image interpretation, and it's roughly a third of the job. In psychiatry, the analogous question is what fraction of the work is information processing that a model could do. Some of it genuinely is — differential diagnosis, drug interactions, documentation, treatment-guideline recall. A model is already better than most humans at all four.

But the thing those tasks surround is not information processing. It's a relationship conducted over months in which the clinician's own reactions are diagnostic instruments. The peripheral role isn't peripheral here. It's the center, and automating around it doesn't shrink it.

**Does lower cost expand the market?** Yes, and this is where the argument gets interesting rather than merely defensive. Cheaper mental health support almost certainly expands demand enormously, because the current unmet need is staggering and much of it goes unaddressed for reasons of cost and access. But expanded demand at the low-acuity end does not substitute for the high-acuity end. It surfaces it. People who begin with an app and discover their problem is larger than an app become people seeking a clinician, and there were never enough clinicians to begin with.

**Is there a regulatory moat?** The deepest in medicine, because it isn't only licensure. It's controlled-substance scheduling, involuntary commitment authority, capacity determinations, mandatory reporting — a set of powers the state has delegated to a licensed human being and shows no interest in delegating to software. Radiology's moat is a credential requirement on a read. Psychiatry's moat includes the legal authority to detain someone against their will, which is not a permission anyone is going to hand to a model.

## Why the centaur model falls short here

There's an argument I hear constantly: layer AI tools on top of existing human skills and you get the best of both. The centaur model.

In many domains that's plainly true, and the last chapter was largely an argument for it. But in deep relational work — in moral judgment, in the kind of presence required when someone is sitting across from you at their worst — AI augmentation doesn't add. It dilutes.

The chatbot therapy studies that exist do reasonably well with mild cases. In anything moderate to severe, outcomes fall apart. And an AI cannot own accountability the way a licensed human must, which is not a software bug awaiting a patch. It's a category difference. Accountability requires something that can be held responsible, and we have not built one of those.

The centaur model works where the work is mostly cognitive and the stakes of being wrong are bounded. In psychiatry neither condition holds.

## The irony

The irony I keep sitting with is that AI will probably generate more demand for psychiatry, not less.

Abundance creates its own crises. Decision paralysis. Meaning collapse. Status anxiety in a world where the old markers of worth — skill, expertise, the salary you could command — compress faster than people can adapt their identities to absorb the hit. There is early evidence linking heavy AI interaction with worse mental health outcomes in some populations, and while I don't want to overstate thin data, the mechanism is not mysterious.

I think we will look back and find that some meaningful part of a coming mental health surge was caused by the same technology that was supposed to liberate us from drudgery. Technology solves problems and creates adjacent ones. That has always been true and there's no particular reason this wave is different in that specific respect.

## What the numbers suggest

Psychiatrist compensation currently sits in a range that has been climbing against chronic, years-long shortages. Mental health roles more broadly are projected to grow substantially faster than the overall economy through the mid-2030s. Meanwhile entry-level software employment in AI-exposed roles has fallen sharply for younger workers. [^25]

The prestige hierarchy is inverting in real time, and I don't think most people making career decisions have noticed. I'm not sure I would have noticed if I weren't looking at it from inside medicine.

I want to flag the shelf life on those numbers honestly. They are the most perishable claims in this book, and the argument does not actually depend on them — it depends on the double moat, which is structural. If the compensation figures moved against me tomorrow, the structural case would still stand, and I'd rather say that now than be caught defending a number later.

## What would make this wrong

If psychiatrist and therapist real wages stagnate over the next decade while AI therapy demonstrates equivalent long-term outcomes in moderate-to-severe cases at mass scale, the thesis weakens considerably. That's the specific falsification, and it's a fair test.

The current data points the other way. I hold it with some humility, because the last chapter's framework is the same framework that says regulatory moats fail when what people want changes underneath them. If enough people decide an always-available, infinitely patient, non-judgmental interlocutor is *preferable* to a human with a waiting list, the moat could dry up the way branch banking's did — not breached, just bypassed.

I don't think that's where this goes, for the reason at the top of the chapter: crisis requires accountability, and accountability requires a person. But I notice that "people won't want that" is exactly what the branch banks believed.

## The dividing line

This is the chapter the book needed, and it's worth saying plainly why.

Everything in Part Two argued that medicine's caution is frequently a reflex that outlived its reason — that scarcity became a virtue and stayed one after the constraint disappeared. If that were the whole story, the correct posture toward institutional resistance would be to discount it uniformly. It isn't, and it shouldn't be.

Psychiatry is where resistance is correct. Not because the field is technologically backward, but because the thing being protected is real: a relationship with legal accountability attached, in which being a person is the active ingredient rather than the delivery mechanism.

So the dividing line the book has been circling looks something like this. **Caution is load-bearing when the human element is the mechanism of the work itself.** Caution is reflex when the human element is merely how the work has historically been delivered.

Radiology's caution is mostly the second kind. The read is a read; the human is currently the instrument that performs it, and the profession's future lies at the frontier where new pathology gets characterized rather than in defending the routine read. Psychiatry's caution is the first kind. You cannot subtract the person and keep the treatment, because in a great deal of psychiatric work the person *is* the treatment.

That distinction is what I'd want a reader to take from this book more than any individual argument about scanners or glucose monitors. Applying it honestly means sometimes concluding that the institution is right and the technologist is wrong — and a critic who can never reach that conclusion isn't offering a diagnosis. He's offering a preference with citations.

The machines will handle more of what we do. The people who matter most in a world where that's true will be the ones helping other people work out who they are. That work requires a human, and some of the humans doing it come with regulatory protection that makes them among the more durable bets in the labor market. Psychiatry has been quietly sitting on this for years.

# The Wedge

Legacy companies and their upstart competitors look like they're chasing different things. They aren't. Both want growth, efficiency, and loyalty. The difference is in *how* they pursue those, and more importantly in what the incumbent is willing to neglect.

The most common blind spot is personalized attention to the customers you already have. Established businesses assume that size, track record, and brand are enough to keep people around. That assumption is precisely the opening a newer competitor uses to wedge in and start pulling customers away, usually without the incumbent noticing until it's well advanced.

This isn't speculation. It's a taught playbook. Founders at incubators are explicitly trained to find these seams — to identify where large companies have grown complacent, attack that specific point, and build momentum from it.

I want to run that playbook against medicine, because the exercise is uncomfortable in a way I think is clarifying. Every reflex this book has described is a seam.

## What the incumbent is neglecting

Take the list.

A patient is told a test isn't necessary, in a sentence that could mean a clinical judgment, a reimbursement rule, or a visit with no room in it — and no way to tell which. A patient asks for their own data and encounters friction dressed as concern. A patient arrives having read something and is met with a flicker of condescension. A patient waits an hour for fifteen minutes. A patient is thoroughly worked up and leaves understanding less than when they arrived.

None of those is a clinical failure. Every one of them is a *relationship* failure, and relationship failures are exactly the seam the playbook targets, because incumbents systematically undervalue them. They don't show up in outcomes data. They don't generate malpractice claims. They're invisible in every metric the institution actually watches.

They are, however, extremely visible to the person experiencing them. And they compound.

The profession's defense has been that the alternative is worse — that the wellness influencer selling supplements is not a substitute for a physician, which is true. But "we are better than the alternative" is the argument every incumbent makes right before losing, and it's usually true right up until it stops mattering. People do not leave because the alternative is better. They leave because the alternative is *interested in them*, and that turns out to be a lower bar than being right.

## What actually defends an incumbent

The good news for medicine is that the defense is known, cheap, and something the profession is already structurally capable of. It isn't innovation. It's community and relationship, and the evidence is sitting inside healthcare already.

I worked at Epic, which runs the software in the large majority of American hospitals and effectively all of the largest ones. Inside their campus the mantra is posted on the walls: *perception equals reality.*

Epic sells software, but the software isn't the moat. The moat is how they cultivate relationships. Their annual user group meeting is closer to a festival than a conference — costumes, spectacle, enormous investment in making it unmissable. They have flown executives out from financially distressed client hospitals specifically to keep them in the room.

Why spend money that way? Because longitudinal relationships are the actual product. Bring clients together year after year and you create belonging, loyalty, shared identity. At that point you aren't a vendor. You're part of how the institution understands itself, and switching costs stop being about software migration.

I want to hold two things about that place at once, because I held both while I was there and I still do.

The community building was genuinely impressive and I learned more from watching it than from anything else I did. Nobody had to explain the strategy to me; you could see it working. Watching a company shape culture at that scale, deliberately, year over year, was the most valuable thing that job gave me.

And the day-to-day was frustrating. Projects that should have taken a day took weeks. There were meetings about meetings. Decisions moved slowly enough that the inertia stopped reading as a bottleneck and started reading as a property of the place. The work itself felt like mastering somebody else's system, and eventually I left to do something else.

Both true, at the same time, about the same company — and the combination is the finding rather than a caveat on it. An organization can be internally slow and externally indispensable simultaneously, because what defends it was never operational speed. It was that the people who buy the product feel known.

Which suggests something uncomfortable: institutional inertia and institutional loyalty may be produced by the same machinery. The deliberateness that makes a company good at long relationships is the same deliberateness that makes it slow to change anything. You probably cannot have one without some of the other, and an incumbent that fixed its slowness by moving fast and breaking things might find it had broken the moat instead.

Hold that before applying any of it to medicine — because medicine is also internally slow, and the question this chapter is really asking is whether it ever built the other half.

Apple does the same thing with its developer conference, and for the same reason. Developers leave not only with technical knowledge but with renewed loyalty — they feel seen. Apple gets mindshare every year for two decades, and it becomes nearly unthinkable for a developer to reprioritize, even during periods when a competitor is genuinely innovating faster.

That's the lesson worth extracting: **innovation alone doesn't produce loyalty. Attention does.**

## Which is the uncomfortable part

Medicine has the strongest possible position for building that kind of loyalty and is squandering it.

No industry has a more intimate relationship with its customers. Physicians are present at births, deaths, diagnoses, and the worst days of people's lives. The raw material for lifelong loyalty is simply handed to the profession, unearned, at every encounter.

And then the system spends it. Not through malice — through the fifteen-minute visit, the screen, the documentation burden, the reflexive no, the information withheld out of a well-meant paternalism. Every one of those is the institution economizing on attention, which is the one thing it cannot economize on without opening the seam.

Epic understood that a hospital executive's sense of being valued was worth flying them across the country for. Medicine has not made the equivalent calculation about patients, because patients are not the customer in the sense that matters to the balance sheet. Chapter Two's question applies with full force here: *who was this designed for?*

## The regulatory moat is not a substitute

The obvious objection is that medicine doesn't need loyalty because it has licensure. You cannot substitute a wellness influencer for a physician when you need surgery. The moat is real, as Chapter Twelve argued at length.

But Chapter Twelve also described how the branch banking moat fell — not because regulators capitulated, but because customer behavior changed so completely that the protection became irrelevant. The moat didn't get breached. The river feeding it changed course.

The equivalent here isn't that people stop needing surgeons. It's that they stop treating the profession as their first call. They self-diagnose, self-treat, buy from someone with better bedside manner and no license, and arrive at the physician later and sicker — still needing medicine, no longer trusting it. The moat holds and the relationship is gone, which is the worst of both outcomes: a profession with guaranteed demand and no authority.

I don't think that's a distant scenario. It takes one company with real distribution and anti-establishment messaging to convert a diffuse feeling of alienation into a market, and the alienation is already there. The people building it don't need to be right about medicine. They need to be more interested in the patient's question than we were.

## Closing the seam

The good news is that closing a seam is a known problem with a known solution, and the solution is not technological.

It's attention. Explaining rather than pronouncing. Handing over information rather than guarding it. Treating a patient's question as a question rather than as noise or as a threat to authority. Being interested.

That is unglamorous, unbillable, and almost entirely unmeasured, which is exactly why it has been neglected and exactly why it's available. The competitors coming for this profession's authority have nothing else. It's the only thing they're offering.

We could simply offer it too. We're already in the room.

# Capitalism vs. Humanitarianism Is a False Choice

Spend enough time around healthcare and you notice two camps.

In one corner, the humanitarians. They care about patients. They believe medicine is a calling. They see healthcare as a moral duty rather than a business, and they are often the people you'd want treating your family.

In the other, the capitalists. They build clinics, design tools, run companies. They talk about incentives, scale, and optimization. Sometimes they make a great deal of money.

The prevailing narrative says you can't be both. You either care or you capitalize. You serve or you scale. You're a good doctor or a greedy founder.

That dichotomy is wrong, and I think it's actively blocking repair. This chapter is where I say why — and it's also where I have to be most careful, because I'm exactly the person this framing implicates, and a chapter arguing that my own position isn't a conflict of interest deserves more skepticism than most.

## Where the split came from

The idea that profit and patient welfare are incompatible isn't arbitrary. It's a reasonable generalization from a real history in which profit frequently came at the expense of people.

And it still does. Hospitals overcharge. Insurers deny coverage. Pharmaceutical companies price drugs at whatever the market will bear. There is no shortage of evidence for extraction, and anyone who waves that away is selling something.

But the existence of extraction doesn't make the *concept* of profit corrupting. It means the system was designed so that extraction pays better than value creation. That's a design failure, and the fix for a design failure is to redesign it so the right thing is also the profitable thing.

That's not idealism. It's an engineering problem, and it only gets solved when people who care about the outcome are willing to build.

## What each side has right

Capitalism, at its best, is a mechanism. It rewards value, scales solutions, and aligns incentive with action.

Most of the modern world's medical infrastructure was built through capital. The devices, the records systems that sometimes work, the vaccine manufactured and distributed globally at a speed nobody had managed before, the companies using computation to detect disease earlier or reach patients in places with no specialist within two hundred miles. These weren't built by governments and nonprofits alone. They were built by companies, by people, often for profit — and the profit didn't make the work less meaningful. It made it *scalable*, which is what let something that worked in one hospital work in a thousand.

Humanitarianism is the necessary correction. It insists that healthcare isn't only a market — that it's a matter of life and death, that dignity and ethics are not externalities, that just because you *can* charge for something doesn't mean you should, and that systems must serve the vulnerable rather than only the profitable.

But humanitarianism unchecked by practical design tends to stall. It avoids discussions about money. It assumes profit always corrupts. It under-resources the very people trying to do the work. And it idolizes the sacrifice of overworked clinicians while ignoring the systems doing the burning — which is a way of moralizing a structural problem so that nobody has to fix it.

The result is a great deal of moral clarity and not nearly enough infrastructure.

## The people worth being

The future of healthcare won't be shaped by the loudest moralists or the richest operators. It'll be shaped by people who can hold both — the compassion to care about the person and the systems thinking to change what happens to the next thousand.

Those people don't just see suffering. They build things that reduce it. They don't only write papers; they turn findings into something that reaches patients. They don't only complain about billing codes; they rebuild the infrastructure underneath them.

The model I'd want is straightforward to state and hard to execute: profit from alignment rather than extraction. Growth through value rather than manipulation. Companies that make money *because* they improve lives rather than despite it.

That's not naive. It's happening in other sectors, and there's no law of nature exempting medicine.

## The part where I implicate myself

Here's the problem with everything I just wrote: it is extremely convenient for me.

I'm a medical student who builds companies. A chapter arguing that commercial interest in medicine is compatible with caring about patients is, transparently, a chapter arguing that I'm fine. The reader should discount it accordingly, and I'd rather say that plainly than let it sit unspoken.

So let me state the strongest version of the objection. Financial interest doesn't announce itself. It works by shaping which problems look interesting, which solutions look promising, and which evidence looks convincing — and it does all of that below the level of conscious reasoning, in people who would sincerely deny being influenced. The physician who owns a stake in an imaging company doesn't order unnecessary scans because he's corrupt. He orders them because the scans genuinely seem warranted to him, and they seem warranted partly because of the stake. That's the whole danger, and it isn't fixed by good intentions.

Which means the honest position isn't that mixed motives are fine. It's that they're *universal*, and the relevant question is what structures make them visible rather than which people can be trusted to transcend them.

Disclosure. Separation between the person recommending an intervention and the person profiting from it. Skepticism proportional to financial stake, applied to me as much as anyone. None of that requires believing that anyone in the arrangement is a bad person, which is why it works better than the alternative of sorting people into the pure and the compromised.

## Why the false choice is expensive

The dichotomy costs something specific, and it's worth naming.

It drives the people with both instincts out of one side or the other. The physician with a systems mind learns that having commercial interests marks him as suspect, so he either suppresses that part or leaves clinical medicine for industry — where he then has no clinical accountability at all, which is a worse outcome for everyone. The builder who cares about patients learns that healthcare is slow, moralizing, and hostile, so she builds something else.

The framing selects against exactly the people best positioned to fix the structural problems this book has spent sixteen chapters describing. And then the profession complains that nobody is fixing them.

Medicine doesn't need more heroes or more hustlers. It needs architects — people who can redesign the structure rather than either enduring it nobly or extracting from it efficiently.

## Which leaves the harder question

Everything above is an argument about systems and roles. It's the easy version, because at the level of systems you can talk about incentive design without ever asking why any particular person does what they do.

The harder version is personal. If mixed motives are universal, and my own are visible enough that I've had to disclose them in the middle of my own book, then what exactly is the difference between someone who helps for good reasons and someone who helps for reasons that happen to pay?

I got asked a version of that question over dinner once, by a friend who thought the answer was that there's no difference at all.

That's the next chapter.

# The Pattern Problem

I had just come from a cardiologist appointment. Two friends were waiting for me at a restaurant, fresh from a workout. I was late — they already had the table and the menus open — but I made it in time to order.

The conversation drifted through the usual territory until one of them posed something that stuck with me.

*I think everyone who helps someone else only does it because there's something in it for them.*

He's religious, and he used religion to make the point. When he helps a stranger, part of him knows he's accumulating blessings, credit in a ledger. The altruism isn't pure. There's always a transaction underneath.

He turned to our other friend. *When's the last time you helped someone out of your way?*

He paused. Thought about it. Couldn't come up with anything recent. And it visibly bothered him — not because the question was unfair, but because it had surfaced something he hadn't wanted to look at.

Then he turned to me.

## The answer I didn't plan

My answer came out before I'd fully thought it through.

*I help people because if I don't, the thought lingers. Like I neglected something I was supposed to do.*

He wanted this to be about religion. But as I explained it further, I realized it wasn't. What I actually felt was that by not helping, I'd be casting a vote — a vote for becoming someone who doesn't help. And that every choice like that, however small, is a vote for the kind of person I'm becoming.

At the time the reframing seemed almost trivial. Dinner moved on. I've thought about it constantly since, because I think it dissolves the question my friend was asking, and it explains a great deal of self-sabotage besides.

## The wrong question

Most people facing a decision ask: *what should I do?*

That framing is almost always wrong, because it treats the decision as isolated. Should I go to the gym today? Should I send that email? Should I help this person? Each choice appears as its own small dilemma, evaluated on its own merits.

But decisions aren't isolated. They're patterned, and patterns compound.

The person who skips the gym *just today* is running an experiment: what happens when I skip? The answer is usually nothing. No punishment. The world continues. So the next time the decision arises, the skip has been validated — slightly easier to choose, not because anything external changed but because a precedent now exists.

That's how patterns form. Not through dramatic choices but through tiny precedents accumulating until they become defaults.

## The right question

The better framing is: *what pattern am I reinforcing?*

That question connects the present choice to a trajectory. It forces you to see the decision not as a point but as a vote in an ongoing election whose winner becomes your identity.

Ask *should I go to the gym today* and you can rationalize a no easily. You're tired. You went yesterday. One day won't matter. All true in isolation.

Ask *what pattern am I reinforcing* and you have to confront something harder. You're either reinforcing the pattern of someone who trains consistently or the pattern of someone who finds reasons not to. There's no third option, because inaction is itself a vote.

## Why this dissolves the motive question

Return to my friend's claim: nobody helps without something in it for them.

On the transactional reading he's right and the observation is deflating. Every act of help can be traced to a return — reputation, blessings, relief from guilt, the pleasant feeling of being the kind of person who helps. If that's the whole story, then moral behavior is just self-interest with better branding, and there's no meaningful difference between the person who helps and the person who doesn't, only different strategies.

The pattern framing gives a different answer without denying any of the facts.

Yes, I get something from helping. What I get is that I become someone who helps. That's not a transaction in any interesting sense — it's the same thing as the act, not a separate payment received in exchange for it. The return isn't external to the behavior. It *is* the behavior, accumulated.

That distinction matters for everything this book has been arguing, because it's the thing that keeps mixed motives from collapsing into cynicism.

## What this has to do with medicine

Chapter Fifteen argued that capitalism and humanitarianism aren't opposed, and then admitted that the argument is extremely convenient for its author. This is the harder version of the same question, taken down to the level of a person.

Medicine tells a story about itself in which the motive is pure service. That story is load-bearing — it's part of why patients extend trust to strangers, and it's genuinely earned by a lot of people.

It's also incomplete in a way everyone inside knows and few say. Everyone in the building is also making a living. There are salary negotiations, productivity targets, compensation formulas that pay more for procedures than for conversations. None of that is scandalous. It's how work works. But because the official story admits no room for it, the ordinary economic facts of a medical career have to be handled in a kind of embarrassed silence, and anything that surfaces them — a physician with a startup, a doctor discussing money publicly — reads as a betrayal of the story rather than as a description of reality.

That silence is expensive. It's why Chapter Five's incentive misalignments went unexamined for so long: you cannot analyze incentives in a profession that isn't allowed to admit it has any. A field that could say plainly *we are motivated by a mix of service, status, curiosity, and money, like every other field, and here is how we structure things so the mix produces good care* would be more honest and considerably harder to corrupt than one insisting the mix doesn't exist.

The pattern framing is what makes that admission survivable rather than corrosive. If motives had to be pure to count, then noticing your own mixed motives would be disqualifying, and the only options would be self-deception or cynicism. Medicine has largely chosen the first. But if what matters is which pattern you're reinforcing, then the mixture is simply the starting condition, and the question becomes what you do with it repeatedly.

## The compounding trap

There's one more piece, and it's the reason the right choice so often feels wrong.

Humans are famously bad at intuiting exponential functions. We feel linear growth accurately — ten dollars a day for a year feels like about three thousand six hundred, and it is. Compound growth breaks the intuition completely. A penny doubled daily for thirty days feels like it should be worth a few dollars. It's over five million.

Here's the part that matters for decisions: exponential curves start *slower* than linear ones. Plot them together and for the first several periods the linear line is ahead. Which means at the moment of choosing, the exponentially better option will often feel worse.

Going to the gym once feels like nothing. Eating a salad feels like something — immediate, tangible, virtuous. Both are good. But the gym compounds in ways the salad doesn't, and the first few sessions may genuinely feel less rewarding than the first few salads.

This is true of almost every high-value behavior. Writing one page feels meaningless next to reading an interesting article. Sending one email feels awkward next to browsing passively. Having one difficult conversation feels worse than letting it slide.

In every case the higher-leverage choice feels worse initially, which suggests a heuristic: among options that are all plausibly good, the one that feels more uncomfortable or more pointless in the short term is often the compounding bet. Not always — some things feel bad because they're bad. But the correlation is strong enough to use.

## Which is why this book exists

I'm aware that writing this is the uncomfortable, apparently pointless choice.

It would be easier to finish training quietly, build things without narrating them, and publish opinions in a decade when they'd carry the weight of experience and cost me nothing. That's the salad. It's genuinely a good option and most people in my position choose it, sensibly.

Saying this out loud now — with two years of clinical experience, in a profession where visibility is coded as a risk-management failure, in a form that can't be quietly edited later — is the version that feels worse. Chapter Four argued that the silence has a cost somebody has to pay. Chapter Three is the record of what happens when you're the one paying it.

I don't know whether it compounds. That's the nature of the bet. What I know is which pattern it reinforces, and that the alternative — waiting for permission, accumulating standing, speaking once it's safe — is also a pattern, and one that has produced a profession that cannot say what it means about its own incentives.

Everyone helps for something. Fine. The question was never whether the motive is pure.

The question is what you're becoming while you answer it.

# What I'd Change

A book that only diagnoses is a complaint with footnotes. At some point the person doing the diagnosing has to say what he would actually do, knowing that saying it makes him accountable for it later.

So here is the part where I stop describing and start committing. I've organized it by how confident I am, from most to least, because the confidence varies a great deal and pretending otherwise would undo the work of the last sixteen chapters.

One standard applies throughout. For each of these I asked the same question: *would this change what medicine is actually like to be on the receiving end of?* Not whether it improves a quality score, or moves a reimbursement metric, or looks good in an annual report — whether the person in the chair would notice. A surprising number of proposals that dominate health policy conversations fail that test, which is part of why the conversations feel so disconnected from the experience of being a patient.

## What I'm confident about

**Separate the rules for gathering information from the rules for taking action.** This is the whole book compressed into a sentence, and it is the change I'd make first if I could make only one. Right now both are governed by a single presumption against excess, inherited from an era when the two moved together. They no longer move together. Codify the split — in training, in guidelines, in what gets taught as good practice — so that "should we collect this?" and "should we act on this?" become separate questions with separate standards of evidence.

From the chair, this is the difference between being told a test isn't necessary and being told what the test would and wouldn't change. The second is a conversation. The first is a verdict, and patients can tell.

**Give patients their own data without friction, and stop treating that as a risk to be managed.** The paternalism here is barely concealed even when well meant. You might misunderstand it, you might worry, you might make a bad decision. Sometimes true, and never a sufficient reason to make information about a person's body harder for them to reach than it is for a stranger with the right login.

I hold this one strongly enough that I should name what would change my mind: if access at scale demonstrably produced worse outcomes — more anxiety-driven utilization, worse adherence, worse health — I'd be wrong, and I'd rather find that out than assume it away. The evidence I've seen doesn't point that direction, but I've also seen how much of what medicine believes about patients turns out to be about what's convenient for medicine.

**Build the interpretation layer instead of using its absence as a reason to refuse data.** The honest objection to abundance isn't philosophical, it's practical: a clinician cannot read a thousand data points in a fifteen-minute visit, and asking them to is an insult dressed as innovation. Fine. Then the work is summarization, pattern surfacing, and interfaces that turn raw traces into a sentence a physician can act on.

This is engineering, not ethics, and it has been treated as impossible for roughly two decades while being merely unbuilt. It is also the piece I'm personally most likely to work on, which the reader should weigh accordingly — I am not a disinterested party here, and Chapter 16 is my attempt to say what that's worth.

**Teach the significant-versus-meaningful distinction in week one, everywhere.** Not as an aside in a biostatistics lecture delivered in the same tone as everything else. It is the single highest-leverage idea in quantitative reasoning, it takes twenty minutes to convey, and its absence produces clinicians who can evaluate whether an effect is real without ever asking whether it matters. Number needed to treat should be as familiar as a p-value and it isn't close.

The patient-side version: this is what stands between someone and a daily medication they'd have declined if anyone had told them the number needed to treat was two hundred.

## What I think is right but can't prove

**Payment reform is the thing that actually moves the culture, and everything else is downstream.** Reimbursement still treats testing as expensive as its real cost collapses, and the architecture of what gets paid for shapes behavior far more reliably than any argument about best practice. Value-based care was supposed to fix this and has mostly produced administrative overhead — which is evidence about implementation rather than about direction, though I hold that view less firmly than I did a year ago.

Capitated models and direct-to-consumer arrangements have the incentive structure closer to correct, because in both the entity paying benefits from the patient not getting sicker. I can't prove this scales. I notice that the people most confident it does tend to be selling something, and I'd rather flag that than pretend I've escaped it.

**Modality-by-modality proof, with symmetric burden.** Each new data source should demonstrate that it improves outcomes net of harms. That's not the complaint — the complaint is that the burden runs one direction. Abundance must prove benefit; scarcity is assumed safe and never tested. Fund the studies that would settle it, and treat "we don't have the evidence" as a reason to generate evidence rather than a reason to decline.

**Stop describing institutional delay as prudence after the fact.** This one is cultural and I have no mechanism for it, which is why it sits in this section rather than the one above. But it's the step that makes each cycle repeat. When a technology takes fifteen years to go from feasible to standard, and the profession's retrospective account is that it was appropriately careful, nothing has been learned that can be applied to the next one. Some institution should keep score honestly — the resistance, the arguments made, the evidence when it arrived, the years lost — because a profession that cannot audit its own delays will keep repeating them and calling it rigor.

## What I don't know how to fix

**The fifteen-minute visit.** Almost every problem in this book runs through it. The clinician who can't absorb more data, the patient who feels dismissed, the documentation burden, the reflexive no — all of them are downstream of a unit of time that is too small for the work assigned to it. I don't have a solution. Every proposal I've seen either costs more than anyone will pay or shifts work onto someone with less training and calls it efficiency.

**Liability.** A physician who orders nothing and misses something faces one kind of exposure. A physician who orders everything and causes a cascade faces another. The asymmetry between those two shapes behavior constantly and invisibly, and no amount of arguing about data categories touches it. Any serious version of the changes above eventually collides with malpractice law, and I know almost nothing about how to move that.

**Whether I'm right about status.** I claimed that clinical acumen is demonstrated through parsimony, and that this creates quiet resistance to tools making diagnosis easier. I believe it. I can't prove it, it's the least charitable claim in the book, and it's exactly the sort of thing that looks obvious from where I'm standing and might look very different after a decade of actually doing the work.

## What I'm going to do about it

I'll be practicing inside this system for decades, which makes all of the above less abstract than it would be coming from someone passing through.

I want to be careful here, because the natural move at this point in a book is to promise something, and I've spent seventeen chapters arguing that people should say what they can actually defend.

So: I can't guarantee I'll keep writing through residency. Anyone who has watched residency happen to a person would be right to doubt it. It is going to be a monumental claim on my time and my attention, and I have no useful evidence about who I'll be on the other side of it.

What I can say is that it's hard for me to imagine not writing, and the reason is mechanical rather than aspirational. I will be reflecting and thinking regardless — that isn't optional, it's what happens in the gaps — and writing is simply how I process. The publishing part is a habit layered on top of a thing I would be doing anyway. Removing it wouldn't free up much, because the underlying activity isn't the writing. It's the thinking, and the writing is only where it becomes legible.

The building is the same answer for a different reason. I can't seem to help taking the next step immediately once I've noticed a problem. And the gap between noticing and having something crude that works has collapsed over the last few years — it used to be weeks, and now it is frequently a few hours.

There's a term for what's happening there and it happens to be a medical one. Positive cooperativity: hemoglobin binds one oxygen molecule and the next one binds more easily, and the next more easily still, because each binding changes the shape of the thing doing the binding. That is the honest description of what building has done to me. Each thing made the next thing cheaper, not because I got dramatically smarter but because the shape changed. The activation energy for starting something is now close to zero, which is a genuinely strange thing to be true about a person and is also the single largest practical difference between me at twenty-two and me now.

Can I promise that pace survives residency? No. Do I currently see a reason it would stop? Also no — and I'd rather leave both of those sitting next to each other than resolve them into a commitment I can't back.

Some people find that kind of ambiguity uncomfortable. I've come to think it's the most useful thing about me. The 50-year plan in Chapter Three was an attempt to eliminate exactly this uncertainty — to specify every step so that nothing depended on who I turned out to be. It was wrong precisely because it was certain.

The one thing I will commit to is the record. I'm going to keep track of what I got wrong. Not as performance, but because the alternative is the thing this entire book has been criticizing: an institution that revises its story afterward so that every delay becomes prudence and nothing is ever learned. If I'm going to ask that of a profession, the least I can do is model it at the scale of one person.

That much I can promise, because it costs nothing but honesty and I've already started.

## The thing I actually believe

Medicine isn't broken. The incentives and structures around it are, and those were designed by people, which means they can be redesigned by people. That's the optimistic reading and I do hold it.

But I want to end on the narrower claim, because it's the one I can defend from here.

The scarcity reflex was a good adaptation to a world of expensive, invasive, genuinely limited tests. That world is going away unevenly — faster in imaging and sequencing, slower in everything requiring a person's hands and time. The reflex is outliving the conditions that justified it, and because it long ago stopped being something a physician does and became something a physician is, it will outlive them by a generation unless someone keeps pointing at the gap.

That's what this book was for. Not to prove that doctors are wrong — mostly they aren't, and the ones resisting are usually protecting patients with the time and tools they have. Just to name a specific inherited reflex, show where it came from, show where it no longer fits, and make the case that the distinction between gathering and acting is worth institutionalizing before another technology spends twenty years waiting for permission.

I'm writing this from a narrow window. In a few years I'll have been trained thoroughly enough that some of what looks strange to me now will look normal, and I won't be able to tell which parts. That's not a reason to distrust the book. It's the reason it exists, and the reason it had to be written now rather than later, by someone who could still see the water.

# Epilogue: What Has Already Changed

I said in the author's note that the window was closing while you read this. Here is the accounting, written at the last possible moment before the manuscript went to print.

The book asks medicine to do something specific and slightly humiliating: to keep score on its own delays, and to stop rewriting them afterward as prudence. It would be indefensible to ask that and not do it here, so this is the part where I check my own work in public.

## Things I have already changed my mind about

**The tone in chapter eleven.** I wrote that I was embarrassed by physicians dismissing a new scanner, and I meant it. I've since spent more time on wards with the people I was describing, and the sentence sits differently now. The physicians I found most reflexive about new technology turned out, on closer acquaintance, to be the ones carrying the most direct memory of harm — the attending who has personally managed the cascade is more cautious than the one who has only read about it, and that is not a character flaw. It's evidence, stored in the only place medicine reliably stores it, which is in people.

I've left the sentence in. It's what I thought, and softening it retroactively would be exactly the move this book criticizes. But I'd write it differently now, and the difference is that I'd aim it at the institution rather than at colleagues.

**How confident I am about payment reform.** In chapter seventeen I filed value-based care under "right but can't prove." I'd move it further down that list now. The more implementations I've looked at closely, the more the administrative overhead looks like a structural feature of measuring outcomes at all rather than a fixable execution problem. I still think fee-for-service is the wrong incentive. I'm less sure anyone knows what the right one is at scale, and I notice the people most confident about it usually have something to sell.

**The status argument.** Chapter six claims that clinical acumen is demonstrated through parsimony, which creates quiet resistance to tools that make diagnosis easier. It's the least charitable claim in the book and I flagged it as such. Having watched more attendings work, I think it's real but much smaller than I implied, and mostly generational. The residents I train with reach for whatever produces the right answer and appear entirely unbothered about how impressive it looks.

## Things I got right and did not expect to

Not many, and I'd rather list the short honest set than pad it.

The information vacuum is worse than I described. Every patient I have seen who arrives having self-diagnosed did so because they could not get a straight answer from someone with a license, not because they preferred the internet. That is not a technology problem and no product will solve it.

The processing layer is still unbuilt. When I wrote that medicine had spent two decades treating an engineering problem as an impossibility, I expected to be somewhat out of date by publication. I am not.

## Things I now know I cannot see

This is the section I most wanted to write, and the hardest.

I can feel my own instincts converging with the people around me. Practices that struck me as strange in my first year have stopped registering, and I cannot reliably tell which of them I've come to understand and which I've merely come to expect. Both feel identical from inside. That symmetry is the whole mechanism this book is named for, and knowing about it turns out to provide almost no protection.

So I have to hand something over here, which is uncomfortable for a book that spends its length arguing.

If you are earlier in this than I am — a first-year, a nurse three months in, someone who came to healthcare from somewhere else and finds it strange — you can currently see things I no longer can. That perception has a short half-life and no institutional home. Nobody will ask you for it. The rewards all run the other direction, toward fitting in, which is mostly correct because fitting in is how you learn to be safe with patients.

Write it down anyway. Not for publication necessarily. Just somewhere dated, so that in five years you can read what you thought before you knew better, and check which parts you resolved and which parts you simply stopped noticing.

That's the only defense I've found against the thing this book describes, and it isn't much of one.

## What the book was for

I'm going to be inside this system for decades. Nothing here was written from the outside, and nothing was written to be clever about a profession I'm leaving.

The argument was narrow on purpose. Medicine built a reflex for scarcity when tests were expensive and invasive and genuinely limited. That world is going unevenly, faster in imaging and sequencing than in anything requiring a person's hands and time, and the reflex is outliving the conditions that produced it. And because it is worn as a professional virtue rather than used as a tool, it will keep outliving them, and each new technology will spend a decade or two waiting for permission while people who could have been helped are not.

The fix is not to abandon restraint. It's to notice that restraint was always about *action*, that it quietly expanded to cover *information*, and that the expansion was never examined because it never had to be.

That's it. That's the book.

Half the room put their hands up the second time, after ninety careful minutes of being told why they shouldn't. My professor read that as a failure of teaching. I've spent seventeen chapters arguing it was a signal, and I'm aware that in a few years I may be standing at the front of a room like that one, watching the hands go up, feeling the disappointment he felt.

If that happens, I hope somebody in the back is taking notes.

— Ali
