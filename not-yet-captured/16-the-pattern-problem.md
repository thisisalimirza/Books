# The Pattern Problem

I had just come from a cardiologist appointment. Two friends were waiting for me at a restaurant, fresh from a workout. I was late — they already had the table and the menus open — but I made it in time to order.

The conversation drifted through the usual territory until one of them posed something that stuck with me.

*I think everyone who helps someone else only does it because there's something in it for them.*

He's religious, and he used religion to make the point. When he helps a stranger, part of him knows he's accumulating blessings, credit in a ledger. The altruism isn't pure. There's always a transaction underneath.

He turned to our other friend. *When's the last time you helped someone out of your way?*

He paused. Thought about it. Couldn't come up with anything recent. And it visibly bothered him — not because the question was unfair, but because it had surfaced something he hadn't wanted to look at.

Then he turned to me.

## The answer I didn't plan

My answer came out before I'd fully thought it through.

*I help people because if I don't, the thought lingers. Like I neglected something I was supposed to do.*

He wanted this to be about religion. But as I explained it further, I realized it wasn't. What I actually felt was that by not helping, I'd be casting a vote — a vote for becoming someone who doesn't help. And that every choice like that, however small, is a vote for the kind of person I'm becoming.

At the time the reframing seemed almost trivial. Dinner moved on. I've thought about it constantly since, because I think it dissolves the question my friend was asking, and it explains a great deal of self-sabotage besides.

## The wrong question

Most people facing a decision ask: *what should I do?*

That framing is almost always wrong, because it treats the decision as isolated. Should I go to the gym today? Should I send that email? Should I help this person? Each choice appears as its own small dilemma, evaluated on its own merits.

But decisions aren't isolated. They're patterned, and patterns compound.

The person who skips the gym *just today* is running an experiment: what happens when I skip? The answer is usually nothing. No punishment. The world continues. So the next time the decision arises, the skip has been validated — slightly easier to choose, not because anything external changed but because a precedent now exists.

That's how patterns form. Not through dramatic choices but through tiny precedents accumulating until they become defaults.

## The right question

The better framing is: *what pattern am I reinforcing?*

That question connects the present choice to a trajectory. It forces you to see the decision not as a point but as a vote in an ongoing election whose winner becomes your identity.

Ask *should I go to the gym today* and you can rationalize a no easily. You're tired. You went yesterday. One day won't matter. All true in isolation.

Ask *what pattern am I reinforcing* and you have to confront something harder. You're either reinforcing the pattern of someone who trains consistently or the pattern of someone who finds reasons not to. There's no third option, because inaction is itself a vote.

## Why this dissolves the motive question

Return to my friend's claim: nobody helps without something in it for them.

On the transactional reading he's right and the observation is deflating. Every act of help can be traced to a return — reputation, blessings, relief from guilt, the pleasant feeling of being the kind of person who helps. If that's the whole story, then moral behavior is just self-interest with better branding, and there's no meaningful difference between the person who helps and the person who doesn't, only different strategies.

The pattern framing gives a different answer without denying any of the facts.

Yes, I get something from helping. What I get is that I become someone who helps. That's not a transaction in any interesting sense — it's the same thing as the act, not a separate payment received in exchange for it. The return isn't external to the behavior. It *is* the behavior, accumulated.

That distinction matters for everything this book has been arguing, because it's the thing that keeps mixed motives from collapsing into cynicism.

## What this has to do with medicine

Chapter Fifteen argued that capitalism and humanitarianism aren't opposed, and then admitted that the argument is extremely convenient for its author. This is the harder version of the same question, taken down to the level of a person.

Medicine tells a story about itself in which the motive is pure service. That story is load-bearing — it's part of why patients extend trust to strangers, and it's genuinely earned by a lot of people.

It's also incomplete in a way everyone inside knows and few say. Everyone in the building is also making a living. There are salary negotiations, productivity targets, compensation formulas that pay more for procedures than for conversations. None of that is scandalous. It's how work works. But because the official story admits no room for it, the ordinary economic facts of a medical career have to be handled in a kind of embarrassed silence, and anything that surfaces them — a physician with a startup, a doctor discussing money publicly — reads as a betrayal of the story rather than as a description of reality.

That silence is expensive. It's why Chapter Five's incentive misalignments went unexamined for so long: you cannot analyze incentives in a profession that isn't allowed to admit it has any. A field that could say plainly *we are motivated by a mix of service, status, curiosity, and money, like every other field, and here is how we structure things so the mix produces good care* would be more honest and considerably harder to corrupt than one insisting the mix doesn't exist.

The pattern framing is what makes that admission survivable rather than corrosive. If motives had to be pure to count, then noticing your own mixed motives would be disqualifying, and the only options would be self-deception or cynicism. Medicine has largely chosen the first. But if what matters is which pattern you're reinforcing, then the mixture is simply the starting condition, and the question becomes what you do with it repeatedly.

## The compounding trap

There's one more piece, and it's the reason the right choice so often feels wrong.

Humans are famously bad at intuiting exponential functions. We feel linear growth accurately — ten dollars a day for a year feels like about three thousand six hundred, and it is. Compound growth breaks the intuition completely. A penny doubled daily for thirty days feels like it should be worth a few dollars. It's over five million.

Here's the part that matters for decisions: exponential curves start *slower* than linear ones. Plot them together and for the first several periods the linear line is ahead. Which means at the moment of choosing, the exponentially better option will often feel worse.

Going to the gym once feels like nothing. Eating a salad feels like something — immediate, tangible, virtuous. Both are good. But the gym compounds in ways the salad doesn't, and the first few sessions may genuinely feel less rewarding than the first few salads.

This is true of almost every high-value behavior. Writing one page feels meaningless next to reading an interesting article. Sending one email feels awkward next to browsing passively. Having one difficult conversation feels worse than letting it slide.

In every case the higher-leverage choice feels worse initially, which suggests a heuristic: among options that are all plausibly good, the one that feels more uncomfortable or more pointless in the short term is often the compounding bet. Not always — some things feel bad because they're bad. But the correlation is strong enough to use.

## Which is why this book exists

I'm aware that writing this is the uncomfortable, apparently pointless choice.

It would be easier to finish training quietly, build things without narrating them, and publish opinions in a decade when they'd carry the weight of experience and cost me nothing. That's the salad. It's genuinely a good option and most people in my position choose it, sensibly.

Saying this out loud now — with two years of clinical experience, in a profession where visibility is coded as a risk-management failure, in a form that can't be quietly edited later — is the version that feels worse. Chapter Four argued that the silence has a cost somebody has to pay. Chapter Three is the record of what happens when you're the one paying it.

I don't know whether it compounds. That's the nature of the bet. What I know is which pattern it reinforces, and that the alternative — waiting for permission, accumulating standing, speaking once it's safe — is also a pattern, and one that has produced a profession that cannot say what it means about its own incentives.

Everyone helps for something. Fine. The question was never whether the motive is pure.

The question is what you're becoming while you answer it.
