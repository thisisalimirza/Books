# Scenes needed

The single highest-value thing only you can do for this manuscript.

Every chapter argues well and most of them argue abstractly. What's missing is
the thing that makes a book like this readable rather than merely correct: a
person in a room, at a specific moment, with details nobody could invent.
Nine of those are listed below.

**Why this is the length fix.** The manuscript is at ~32,000 words against a
76,500 target. These nine scenes at 400–800 words each are roughly 5,000
words on their own — but they do more than that, because a real scene gives
the surrounding argument something to refer back to, and chapters expand
naturally around a concrete anchor. Padding the prose would add the same
word count and make the book worse. This adds the same word count and makes
it better.

**Ground rules.** Write these as they happened, including the parts that
don't flatter the argument. If a scene turns out to contradict the chapter it
sits in, that's a finding — tell me and I'll rework the chapter around it,
which is what happened with the Korea material in Chapter Eleven.

**On patient material:** anything from a real clinical encounter needs proper
de-identification and, where required, consent. See `writing-plan.md` §6.5.
Composite or heavily altered details are fine if you say so in the author's
note; inventing an encounter is not.

## 1. Ch6 — Information that existed and couldn't be used
**Priority: highest.** This is the book's most important missing scene.

Chapter Six argues that medicine has abundance at the collection layer and
scarcity at the interpretation layer, and that this is an infrastructure
problem rather than a principled one. Right now that argument is asserted.

What I need is one moment from rotations where the information was *there*
and could not be used. A chart containing everything and a visit with no time
to read it. A monitor alarming into an empty hallway. A patient arriving with
a year of wearable data that nobody opened, and what was said to them.

400–600 words. The room, the timing, what you thought at the time rather than
what you concluded later.

## 2. Ch9 — The patient who was fully worked up and not understood
**Priority: highest.**

Chapter Nine's clinical argument — that decomposing a person into a problem
list can lose the person — currently has no instance. One patient, thoroughly
investigated, every system examined, and still nobody had assembled a picture.

The useful version includes what the reassembly would have required and why
nobody did it. Not negligence. Time.

400–600 words.

## 3. Ch11 — Your grandmother
**Priority: high. Handle as you want to.**

The seed essay says your grandmother died because her cancer was found too
late, and the chapter currently uses that in a single sentence. It's the
emotional center of your whole argument about early detection and it's
carrying almost no weight on the page.

If you're willing to write it properly, it changes the chapter. If you'd
rather it stay one line, say so and I'll leave it — this is not a scene

## 4. Ch14 — Epic
**Priority: high. Highest-value non-clinical material in the book.**

You worked at the company whose software runs most American hospitals. That's
an insider vantage almost nobody writing about health technology has, and the
chapter currently uses two details: the mantra on the bathroom wall, and the
user group meeting.

What I'd want: what it was actually like inside. What the culture optimized
for. A specific thing you watched them do that explains why hospitals don't
switch. Whether the "perception equals reality" line was cynical or sincere
in practice.

**Check anything non-public against your agreements before writing.** If most
of it is covered, tell me and I'll rebuild the chapter around public
information.

## 5. Ch2 — Reading the incentive over the stated reason
**Priority: medium.**

Chapter Two names four analytical habits business installed. The first —
read the incentive before the stated reason — needs one moment from the
agency years where doing that changed what you did. A client whose stated
request wasn't the real request. A negotiation where the money explained
what the words didn't.

300–500 words. It doesn't have to be a triumph; a case where you read it
wrong and learned would work as well.

## 6. Ch5 — The first time you heard "the system is broken"
**Priority: medium.**

The chapter opens with the observation that everyone says this in the tone
people use for weather. Who said it first, where, and what were they doing at
the time? The casualness is the point.

200–300 words.

## 7. Ch8 — The biostatistics lecture, at full length
**Priority: medium. Expansion rather than a new scene.**

The exchange with the professor is already the best-written moment in the
book and it runs about a page. It could run three.

What's missing is the room: the slide, the sound of everyone else still
typing, how long you sat there before raising your hand, what his face did
when you approached him afterward, whether anyone else stayed behind. You
described him as making peace with the failure — that's the detail worth
extending.

+400–600 words.

## 8. Ch13 — The psychiatry rotation
**Priority: medium.**

The chapter opens with a voice note recorded on the way to the rotation and
then leaves the rotation entirely. One encounter that made the double-moat
argument concrete — a moment where being a person was clearly the active
ingredient rather than the delivery mechanism — would ground the whole
chapter.

Consent and de-identification apply. 300–500 words.
