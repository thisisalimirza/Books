# Turn the Volume up Doctor

There is a brief moment in an old *[My First Million](https://www.mfmpod.com/)* episode that captures the exact fault line between the entrepreneurial world and the medical establishment.

Sam Parr, one of the co-hosts, was recounting a personal period of founder paralysis—that specific inertia where you have the capital (time or money) and the energy but can’t decide what to build next. He mentioned it to his trainer, who stopped him mid-sentence:

> *“You are the product. The product is just you pushed out. So just do you, but turn the volume knob up.”*

If you are a founder, a software engineer, or a marketer, that sentence feels like a green light you were already looking for, and might have already suspected was coming up. It means stripping away the corporate abstraction and relying on raw, authentic leverage. You know it works because you see it online all the time. Examples like Roy Lee, Zach Yadegari, and Bryan Johnson are just some of the few relevant examples these days.

But if you are a physician, that sentence sounds like a structural threat. It triggers an immediate, visceral Type 1 hypersensitivity allergic reaction in you.

## The Institutional Cult of Anonymity

Medicine trains the instinct of invisibility on purpose, and the conditioning begins on day one of medical school.

Consider Joel Bervell, a medical student who began producing short-form videos exposing clinical blind spots, like racial biases in pulse oximetry and lung function algorithms. It was objectively vital public health data—exactly the kind of evidence-based translation the public desperately needs. Yet, his institutional advisors warned him to “think twice” before speaking publicly about “hot-button issues.”

The warning wasn’t about the accuracy of his data. It was about the fact that he was visible at all. In the legacy medical paradigm, **visibility itself is treated as a risk-management failure.**

Dr. Eric Topol, a well regarded physician who writes about modern technology and trends in medicine, notes that this systemic suppression starts in the exam room long before it reaches the internet. We are systematically conditioned to maintain a sterile emotional distance from patients under the guise of objective professionalism.

The traditional system wants the physician to be a blank, interchangeable node. A white coat, a stethoscope, and an institutional logo. No edge, no distinct perspective, and absolutely no volume.

## The Cost of Our Silence

This institutional obsession with compliance isn’t just outdated—it has become dangerous.

When the medical establishment codes silence as a virtue, it doesn’t stop people from looking for medical advice. It simply ensures they won’t get it from us. By treating public visibility as a professionalism violation, medicine created a massive information vacuum.

And vacuums are always filled.

```
[Institutional Silence] ──> [Information Vacuum] ──> [Unregulated Wellness Grift]
```

Because actual physicians are terrified of institutional friction, the public square has been surrendered to biohackers, wellness influencers, and charlatans selling unvetted supplements and $35 EMS machines. They have zero clinical training, but they have complete mastery over the volume knob.

When a licensed physician decides that staying invisible is the “safe” career move, they aren’t protecting patients. They are outsourcing the public’s health education to the loudest, least-qualified voices on the internet.

Invisibility is no longer a neutral ethical stance. It is a dereliction of duty.

## The Alignment Test: Reorganizing Authenticity

To fix this, we need a better framework for what “professionalism” actually means online. The current standard is arbitrary: if you write a peer-reviewed paper that three people read, you are a scientist; if you summarize that exact paper in plain English for 100,000 people, you are a self-promoting risk.

A more rigorous definition comes from investor Alex Hormozi, who defines authenticity through a brilliant thought experiment: **How would you behave if there were zero possibility of punishment or reward?**

Authenticity is the total collapse of the public and private persona. There is no delta between who you are when the microphones are off and who you are when they are live.

For a physician looking to scale their voice, this offers a brutally clarifying filter. It replaces vague institutional hand-wringing with two simple questions:

- **The Clinical Alignment Test:** *Would I say this exact same thing to a patient sitting across from me in an exam room, using my genuine clinical judgment?* If the answer is yes, then keeping silent online isn’t professional discipline—it’s just fear wearing the costume of ethics.
- **The Performance Filter:** *Am I only saying this online because it performs an optimization for an algorithm or projects a confidence I don’t actually possess?* If the answer is yes, *that* is the real professional violation. It has nothing to do with volume, and everything to do with deceit.

## Scaling the Product

Sam Parr has a second test for authenticity: the “yes test.” Would you do this specific work for free, or even at a loss, simply because it is the truest expression of what you believe?

That is the standard we should hold ourselves to. When I write *Side Effects*, I don’t approach it as a marketing exercise or a personal branding play. I write it because the intersection of technology, medicine, and systems thinking is exactly where my mind goes when nobody is paying me to think. I would write it if the subscriber count was zero.

The credentials on your wall were never meant to be a shield to hide behind. They were meant to be a foundation to build upon.

The physicians who are actually changing the landscape right now aren’t inventing a persona for an audience. They are taking the exact clinical judgment, clear communication, and lack of jargon they use to save lives in the clinic, and they are pushing it outward.

Stop letting institutional inertia dictate your reach. The product is your judgment. Turn the volume up.
