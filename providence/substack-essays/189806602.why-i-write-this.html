I was twelve years old the last time I moved countries.

By that point I’d lived in Pakistan, Lebanon, Kenya, and then America. Four countries before middle school. I didn’t choose any of it — you don’t, when you’re a kid — but it did something to my brain that I’ve never fully been able to undo: it made it impossible for me to believe that the way things were done was the only way things could be done.

Every place had a different set of rules. Different things that were obvious. Different things that were unspeakable. And because I’d watched those rules change completely every few years, I could never fully commit to any of them. I was always, at some level, a visitor. Watching. Taking notes.

That’s still how I move through the world. I’m a second-year medical student. I’m also building companies. I’ve managed an investment portfolio for friends or family since I was in college. I shut down a six-figure agency to come to medical school, and I’ve been rebuilding in the cracks ever since — between flashcard sessions, before the morning lecture, on the Amtrak back from a client meeting I probably shouldn’t have taken.

The people around me in medical school think this is unusual. The people I know in startups think medical school is unusual. Everybody thinks something about it.

I’ve stopped explaining.

This newsletter exists because I kept noticing a gap.

Not between medicine and business — that gap is obvious and everyone talks about it. The deeper gap between how institutions explain themselves and what they actually are. Between what you’re told you’re learning and what you’re actually being trained to do. Between the official story and the incentives underneath it.

I paid over $200,000 for an undergraduate education (in theory; in practice, I had a scholarship). I took statistics. I got good grades. And nobody — not once, in four years — told me that “statistically significant” and “clinically meaningful” are completely different questions. My biostatistics professor said it in passing in my first year of medical school. My classmates kept writing. I stopped mid-sentence, hand frozen, because I realized something was broken.

That moment is why I write.

Not because I want to expose institutions. Not because I’m cynical about systems. But because the gap between what things are supposed to do and what they actually do is where almost everything interesting happens. It’s where the leverage is. It’s where the blindspots are. And it’s where most people — smart, serious, hardworking people — keep getting surprised by outcomes they didn’t see coming.

I call these the side effects.

Here’s what I’ve learned from living inside multiple systems at once:

Every system has a story it tells about itself, and a different story visible only from the outside. Medical school tells you it’s training healers. It’s also a credential machine, a socialization process, and a filter designed to select for tolerance of authority. Both things are true. The official story isn’t a lie — it’s just incomplete.

Business tells you it rewards value creation. It also rewards narrative, timing, and access to capital. The market doesn’t just find the best product. It finds the best product that the right people heard about at the right moment, with the right story attached.

Most advice is optimized for the person in the middle of the bell curve. It’s designed for someone who has one career, one goal, one arena. If you’re trying to do two things seriously — medicine and building, or law and writing, or finance and something else entirely — almost none of the standard advice fits. You’re not optimizing for the same variables. You’re not playing the same game.

The people who figure this out early tend to move faster. Not because they’re smarter, but because they stopped waiting for permission from systems that weren’t designed for them.

I’m not going to tell you I have it figured out.

I have $350,000 in medical school debt and roughly $10,000 invested. I shut down a business that was compounding at 65% annually because I couldn’t maintain quality while in school — and I spent months afterward realizing I’d misread my own results the entire time, thinking I was failing when I was doing something remarkable. I’m bad at staying in touch with people I care about. My schedule looks insane and mostly doesn’t work.

But I’ve also been in rooms I wasn’t supposed to be in. I’ve closed deals I had no business closing. I’ve watched the pattern repeat enough times — in enough different industries, countries, and institutions — that I’ve started to trust what I see. Not because my sample size is large enough for statistical significance. But because the underlying structure keeps showing up.

That structure is what I write about.

Side Effects is for people who are building something while navigating a system that wasn’t designed for them.

The medical student with a startup idea. The consultant who wants out. The resident applying for fellowships who’s also quietly writing a book. The person who took the traditional path because it made sense at the time, and now finds themselves wondering what the actual rules are — the ones nobody wrote down.

If that’s you, you’ll feel at home here.

I write when I have something to say, not on a schedule. Every essay is an attempt to think clearly about something I’m living through or can’t stop thinking about. Some are about medicine. Some are about money and markets. Some are about ambition, and what it costs, and whether it’s worth it.

All of them are about the gap between the official story and what’s actually true.

Welcome. I’m glad you’re here.

— Ali
