# Refunding $1,100 Hurt, and Here's Why

I refunded $1,100 last week—not because I wanted to, but because I had no choice. It was one of those gut-punch moments that made me question not just my decision-making, but my entire approach to client management.

Here's exactly what happened:

A client hired my agency, JANUS, for a motion-design video project. Things started off as usual—invoice sent, deposits were paid, and the creative process kicked off.

Then, halfway through, once we delivered the initial draft, the client delayed their feedback by around two weeks. Initially, I brushed off the delay as typical client behavior. Eventually, by the time they responded, my contractor's revision window had already passed and now I’d have to pay the contractor money I didn’t have just for them to make the requested revisions. Instead of facing the issue head-on, I hesitated. I avoided what felt like an awkward conversation, hoping the issue would somehow resolve itself. Extremely stupid.

Of course, it didn't.

Days turned into weeks, the trust we'd built eroded, and eventually, the only honorable solution left was a full refund. To make maters worse, I had recently setup new spam filters in my inbox and somehow the multiple Stripe (my payment processor) emails from the client eventually rightfully requesting a refund were all being filtered out— I never saw any of them and just kept ignoring the problem.

It was a costly mistake, and series of excessively idiotic mistakes on my part. Not just financially but emotionally. I feel a profound sense of embarrassment, frustration, and a genuine hit to my self-respect as a business owner. Those know me know that very, very few things can get to me. There could be an explosion next to me and I \*might\* turn to look, but no guarantees. Yet, somehow, in this moment, with everything else going on, I chose ignorance and ruined a relationship.

### Poor Communication Creates "Bad" Clients

This experience forced me to confront a harsh truth: The narrative around "bad clients" is often misguided.

We frequently label clients as difficult or unreasonable, but most of the time, the root of these problems lies in our own poor communication, unclear expectations, or weak boundary-setting.

In my case, it was easy to blame the client’s delayed response. But the real problem was my failure to establish clear processes for feedback and revision windows upfront. My lack of assertiveness allowed the project to spiral, ultimately leading to the refund scenario.

### The 3-Step Client Communication Safety Net

Determined never to let this happen again, I’ve developed a straightforward but robust three-step system that ensures clarity and keeps client relationships strong:

1. **Kickoff Document:**  
   Before any project starts, every client should receive a clear, detailed kickoff document outlining exact deliverables, timelines, responsibilities, and specifically defined revision windows. Until now I’ve tried avoiding these things, seeing them as formalities and hurdles in the what should be a trust-driven sale. I’m realizing even when there is trust, it's critical to have a roadmap that everyone can reference, ensuring mutual accountability from day one.
2. **Pause Clause:**  
   This addition to all contracts clearly stipulates what happens if client feedback is delayed beyond an agreed-upon timeframe. It specifies potential delays in delivery dates, additional re-engagement fees, or even the temporary suspension of the project until the client is ready to proceed. This simple clause has become a powerful tool to protect both my agency and my contractors.
3. **48-hour Edit Response Deadline:**  
   A strict 48-hour deadline for edit feedback ensures projects stay on schedule. This deadline is communicated clearly and regularly reinforced. By making rapid, actionable feedback part of the client’s responsibilities, projects flow smoothly, and revisions remain within manageable limits.

### Systems, Not Personalities

Here's my core insight from this painful experience: Client problems typically aren't about personalities—they're about systems. When projects go awry, it's easy to take things personally and blame difficult client behavior. But more often, these conflicts emerge because we failed to set clear expectations or avoided addressing issues early enough.

Hard conversations only become harder the longer you wait. Clear, structured communication from the outset prevents misunderstanding, preserves trust, and ultimately saves you from costly financial and emotional setbacks.
