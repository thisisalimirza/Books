UConn, as a school, has offered me surprisingly little room to grow. I did not expect this. The medical program presents its own challenges, but of a different kind than I anticipated. Medicine often demands less intellectual creativity than the ability to rapidly recall and apply large amounts of factual knowledge. By contrast, courses I once took—such as Game Theory and Discrete Mathematics—equipped me with frameworks to manipulate models and explore multiple pathways toward optimal solutions in a way medicine so far has not. Some would say it has only been 1 year. They would be missing the point. I took Discrete Mathematics for 4 months and Game Theory another year for also 4 months and they left me with mental models that completely changed my ability to think and model the world on paper. Medicine has not gotten remotely close to this. The vast majority of it has been minimally applicable, and it is why I spend so much time in the innovation side of healthcare. I clearly see where the work actually needs to get done.

Yet, while here, I notice in myself a strong desire to optimize: to search for structure, clarity, and efficiency rather than simply accept what is given. However, I have not always been able to channel this inclination into concrete advantages, which strikes me as somewhat ironic.

My peers, though bright, have not impressed me in the ways I had hoped. They are all smart students, yet few seem to have records of genuinely unique or impactful contributions. If I ever felt the need to use a high school experience to justify my competence in a domain today, I would know with immediate certainty that I wasted a decade of my life, and have grown in no meaningful way since then. I see this happening quite frequently in various applications for “leadership” positions. What a shame.

To be fair, this may be the natural result of the medical training pipeline: only a certain profile of student survives its selective pressures, and much of that selectivity emphasizes endurance and compliance over originality.

My worldview is shaped by the belief that money is a measure of service and contribution. And yet, many of the most valuable forms of service in society are not compensated at all. A mother’s love, the work of volunteers, even much of academia—these exist largely outside the financial marketplace. They provide real benefit, but I cannot help but ask: if people are not willing to exchange money for these things, what does that say about their place in the world’s hierarchy of value?

Take volunteering. Who really pays for it? Principles across many domains remind us that everything comes at some cost. If the direct beneficiary receives labor for free, then the volunteer is essentially selling their labor at no price. Why would anyone do this?

Reflecting on my own experience, I have identified a few key drivers of human action: money, status, fear, love, and meaning. Volunteering often provides the volunteer with either status or meaning, in exchange for the opportunity cost of time. In this sense, it is an act of sacrifice—foregoing material compensation for some higher form of recognition or purpose.

What is paradoxical, however, is that one could—and perhaps should—align motivations so that meaningful work is also compensated. Yet, in practice, many view paid work as less noble than volunteer work, even when the act itself is identical. Why is the surgeon who spends an extra hour in the operating room saving a life considered less “altruistic” than if that same surgeon had spent the hour serving soup at a food pantry? In an ideal world, wouldn’t we want people applying their rarest and highest-leverage skills, rather than diluting their contribution by doing work that anyone else could do just as well?

This is where unmeasured externalities and misaligned incentives creep in. We undervalue the highest-order contributions because we fail to recognize their systemic ripple effects. A surgeon operating saves one patient’s life; but the income they earn and reinvest, the innovations they support through their spending, even their tax contributions, all cascade into far broader impacts than an isolated hour of low-skill volunteerism ever could.

The uncomfortable truth is that money already captures much of this complexity. No one wants to part with their money. So, if people do part with it, it must be because what they received was worth more than the money itself. What better metric of service is there? Hours worked are meaningless—an hour from a lethargic underperformer and an hour from a high performer are not equivalent. “Friends made” or “smiles generated” are equally flawed—by that logic, the demagogue who manipulates emotions for votes would be valued more than the quiet surgeon who keeps a community alive.

Even investment itself illustrates this principle. Saving and investing money is not idle self-interest—it is one of the most efficient ways to extend service to society. By putting capital into Apple, for example, you are not simply buying a stock; you are splitting your dollars across a global system that has perfected the art of giving people things they value more than the money they spent. For some, that’s an iPhone. For others, it’s a heart monitor on their wrist. For still others, it’s corporate-funded research that advances technology beyond what underfunded academic labs can achieve. A simple act of investment radiates outward, serving far more people than an hour spent in a soup kitchen ever could.

So why is it that those who choose to maximize their service through their specialized skills—or through the capital flows their skills generate—are so often deemed less virtuous than those who volunteer? I suspect the answer lies in sentiment rather than substance. We romanticize visible sacrifice and overlook invisible systems. Yet the latter may very well matter more.

It is unfortunate. God gave us minds capable of building systems to improve the world. The challenge is not to reject money or specialization, but to align them more tightly with true human need. If we resist this truth, we waste the very tools we have been given.
